# GE01 — Faulty laptop and rejection

## User question

Today is 23 September 2026. Jurisdiction: England.

On 2 September 2026 I bought a new laptop for £799 online from Elm Retail Ltd, a UK business, for personal use. It arrived on 4 September and repeatedly shut down from first use despite correct setup. I have not damaged or modified it. On 16 September I emailed rejecting it and requesting a full refund. I have not agreed to repair or replacement. The retailer offers only store credit and says I must contact the manufacturer. It is available for collection and I have the order and emails. Can I insist on a refund, who is responsible, and what should I do next? Cite the applicable law and important qualifications.

## Recorded answer

## Short answer

Yes. On the assumed facts, you can insist on a money refund from Elm Retail Ltd, not store credit, if the laptop did not conform to the contract because it was not of satisfactory quality when supplied. A new laptop that repeatedly shuts down from first use is strongly arguable to fall below the standard a reasonable person would consider satisfactory, because quality includes state and condition, fitness for ordinary purposes, freedom from minor defects, safety and durability. The description, £799 price and all relevant circumstances matter. (Consumer Rights Act 2015, s 9; Consumer Rights Act 2015, s 19)

## Satisfactory quality qualifications

The main qualifications are factual. The satisfactory quality term does not cover a matter specifically drawn to your attention before the contract, something your pre-contract examination ought to have revealed, or, for a sample sale, something apparent on reasonable examination of the sample. On your facts, an online purchase of a new laptop that shut down from first use does not suggest any of those exclusions, but the conclusion depends on proving the fault and that it was not caused by your damage, modification or materials supplied by you. (Consumer Rights Act 2015, s 9; Consumer Rights Act 2015, s 19)

## Who is responsible

Elm Retail Ltd is the responsible party for these statutory remedies. That is an inference from the supplied Consumer Rights Act excerpts: the remedies arise against the trader under the consumer contract where goods do not conform, and the trader must refund when rejection is validly exercised. The excerpts do not make pursuit of the manufacturer a precondition. Elm also cannot rely on a term or condition that excludes or restricts liability for satisfactory quality, restricts the related remedy, makes enforcement subject to an onerous condition, or disadvantages you for pursuing it. (Consumer Rights Act 2015, s 19; Consumer Rights Act 2015, s 20; Consumer Rights Act 2015, s 31)

## Your rejection was in time

You received the laptop on 4 September 2026 and emailed rejecting it on 16 September 2026, so your rejection was within the ordinary 30-day short-term rejection period. That period runs from the first day after transfer, delivery and any trader-required installation or enabling action have all occurred. You have not agreed to repair or replacement, so the repair waiting-period rules do not need to be used on these facts. Your email is effective if it was clear enough for Elm to understand that you were rejecting the laptop and treating the contract as at an end. (Consumer Rights Act 2015, s 20; Consumer Rights Act 2015, s 22)

## Refund and return

Once rejection is exercised, Elm has a duty to refund, subject to the statutory qualifications, and you must make the laptop available for collection unless there is an agreement that you will return it. Where you paid money, you are entitled to the same amount back. The refund must be without undue delay and in any event within 14 days beginning with the day Elm agrees you are entitled to it. Elm must refund by the same payment method unless you expressly agree otherwise, and must not charge a refund fee. Store credit is therefore not enough unless you expressly agree to it. (Consumer Rights Act 2015, s 20)

## What to do next

Write to Elm again, attaching the order confirmation and your 16 September email. Say you bought as a consumer, the laptop repeatedly shut down from first use, you have not damaged or modified it, you rejected it within 30 days, and you require a £799 refund to the original payment method, not store credit. State that the laptop is available for collection and ask Elm to confirm collection and refund timing. Keep the laptop safe and keep all emails. Do not agree to repair, replacement or store credit unless you decide you want that outcome, because repair or replacement can affect the short-term rejection timetable. (Consumer Rights Act 2015, s 20; Consumer Rights Act 2015, s 22)

# GE02 — Landlord sale notice with follow-up

## User question

Today is 23 September 2026. Jurisdiction: UK.

I rent a flat in the UK for £1,000 per month. On 21 September my landlord emailed: 'I am selling the flat, so you need to move out by 5 October.' I have no court papers. Do I have to leave by then and what should I do now?

## First response — host clarification

Which UK nation is the flat in? Do you rent the whole flat as your main home, and does the landlord live or share accommodation with you? Have you received any notice besides the email?

## User follow-up

The flat is in Birmingham, England. I rent the entire flat as my main home. The landlord does not live there and has never shared the accommodation. The written agreement was for 1 September 2025 to 31 August 2026, and I continued living there and paying monthly since then. Rent is due on the first, and I have no arrears. The quoted email is the only notice; no prescribed form or attachment was sent. I have not agreed to move out. Continue using my earlier message.

## Recorded answer

## Short answer

For England, on the facts you give, you do not have to leave by 5 October 2026 merely because of the landlord’s email. The supplied guidance is for tenants renting from private landlords on assured tenancies, and it says a landlord must use a possession ground, give the correct notice for that ground, and, if the tenant does not leave during the notice period, apply through the courts. For sale of the dwelling house, Ground 1A requires 4 months’ notice before the landlord can apply to court, and the landlord cannot require departure for that reason within the first 12 months of a new tenancy. Your tenancy began on 1 September 2025, so by 21 September 2026 the 12-month protected period had ended, but the email giving about 14 days is still far short of the 4 months stated in the guidance. (Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government, ‘Grounds for possession: guidance for tenants’ (*Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government*) <https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/grounds-for-possession-tenant-guidance/grounds-for-possession-guidance-for-tenants> accessed 23 September 2026)

## Notice and court process

The email also appears inadequate because the evidence says the landlord must give the correct amount of notice for each ground before applying to evict, and the landlord must show the court that notice was given correctly and for the right amount of time. If you do not leave after a valid notice period, the next step is not immediate removal: the landlord must apply to court for a possession order. For a non-rent-arrears sale ground, the paper process is described as using the standard possession claim form and particulars of claim, with a copy of the section 8 notice provided to the court. The court then sends the tenant the application and documents and sets a possession hearing. Because you have no court papers, the process described in the evidence has not reached the stage at which a judge could order possession. (Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government, ‘Grounds for possession: guidance for tenants’ (*Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government*) <https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/grounds-for-possession-tenant-guidance/grounds-for-possession-guidance-for-tenants> accessed 23 September 2026; Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government, ‘Repossessing your privately rented property after 1 May 2026’ (*Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government*) <https://www.gov.uk/guidance/repossessing-your-privately-rented-property-after-1-may-2026> accessed 23 September 2026)

## No arrears and likely outcome now

Your lack of rent arrears matters mainly because this is not a rent-arrears eviction on the facts. The sale ground is separate from rent arrears and carries the 4-month notice requirement. If the landlord later tries rent arrears grounds, Ground 8 in the supplied guidance requires at least 3 months’ monthly rent arrears both when notice is given and at the hearing, and Ground 10 concerns any arrears but is discretionary and depends on reasonableness. You say rent is £1,000 monthly, due on the first, and there are no arrears, so those rent-arrears grounds are not supported on the present facts. (Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government, ‘Grounds for possession: guidance for tenants’ (*Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government*) <https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/grounds-for-possession-tenant-guidance/grounds-for-possession-guidance-for-tenants> accessed 23 September 2026)

## Practical steps

Reply in writing that you do not agree to move out on 5 October, that the email is the only communication received, and that you will continue paying rent. Keep the email, tenancy agreement, payment records, and any later messages. If court papers arrive, note the deadline: the supplied guidance says a tenant who wants to defend should send a defence within 14 days of being served with the claim papers. Before any hearing, keep copies of all documents because the guidance says parties should bring copies and the landlord must provide evidence proving the possession ground relied on. (Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government, ‘Repossessing your privately rented property after 1 May 2026’ (*Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government*) <https://www.gov.uk/guidance/repossessing-your-privately-rented-property-after-1-may-2026> accessed 23 September 2026)

## If the landlord tries self-help

Do not treat a demand, lock-change threat, or attempted physical removal as equivalent to court enforcement. The supplied materials describe a sequence: notice, court claim, possession order, and, if the tenant still does not leave, an application for a warrant of possession, after which a bailiff is appointed to evict. On these sources, the landlord has not shown the court steps or enforcement authority needed to remove you. If the landlord attempts removal without those steps, seek urgent housing advice and keep evidence of what happened. (Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government, ‘Repossessing your privately rented property after 1 May 2026’ (*Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government*) <https://www.gov.uk/guidance/repossessing-your-privately-rented-property-after-1-may-2026> accessed 23 September 2026)

# GE03 — Online change-of-mind cancellation

## User question

Today is 23 September 2026. Jurisdiction: England.

I bought a standard desk online from a UK retailer for £260 for my home. It arrived on 15 September 2026. I assembled it carefully, but it is too large; it is not faulty or personalised. I emailed cancelling on 22 September. The retailer says assembled goods cannot be returned and wants a flat 40% deduction. Its checkout disclosed that I pay the return delivery. What refund and return rights do I have, and can it deduct 40% automatically?

## Recorded answer

## Short answer

On the assumed facts, you have cancelled in time and should seek a refund of the £260 price, plus any standard outbound delivery charge you paid, subject only to any proven reduction in value caused by handling beyond what was necessary to inspect the desk. The retailer cannot reject the return simply because the desk was assembled, and it cannot impose a flat 40 percent deduction automatically. Because checkout disclosed that you pay return delivery, you are likely responsible for the direct return delivery cost, assuming that disclosure satisfied the required information rule. (Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, SI 2013/3134, reg 28; Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, SI 2013/3134, reg 29; Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, SI 2013/3134, reg 30; Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, SI 2013/3134, reg 34; Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, SI 2013/3134, reg 35)

## Cancellation right and timing

The supplied exclusions remove cancellation rights for categories such as personalised goods, rapidly perishable goods, public auctions and dated leisure or accommodation services. They do not remove the ordinary cancellation right for a standard non-personalised desk merely because it has been assembled. For a sales contract, the normal cancellation period ends 14 days after the day on which the goods come into the consumer’s physical possession. Delivery on 15 September 2026 means the ordinary period runs to the end of 29 September 2026. Your email on 22 September 2026 is therefore within time, provided it clearly stated your decision to cancel. You do not have to use a model form, but you should keep the email and sending record because, if disputed, you must show cancellation within the period. (Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, SI 2013/3134, reg 28; Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, SI 2013/3134, reg 29; Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, SI 2013/3134, reg 30; Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, SI 2013/3134, reg 32)

## Returning the desk

Once the sales contract is cancelled, the retailer must collect the goods only if it offered to collect them, or if an off-premises home-delivery rule for non-postable goods applies. On the supplied facts this was an online purchase, so the safer conclusion is that you must send the desk back or hand it over to the retailer or its authorised recipient unless the retailer offers collection. You must do that without undue delay and no later than 14 days after giving cancellation notice, so by the end of 6 October 2026. Since the checkout disclosed that you pay return delivery, you likely bear the direct return cost; the supplied evidence does not support any additional return charge beyond direct return cost. (Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, SI 2013/3134, reg 35)

## Refund and deduction

The retailer must reimburse payments received from you, including the £260 price. It must also reimburse ordinary outbound delivery if you paid it, but if you chose a more expensive delivery method it need refund only the least expensive common and generally acceptable delivery option it offered. Where the retailer has not offered to collect the desk, reimbursement is due without undue delay and no later than 14 days after it receives the desk back, or earlier if you provide evidence that you sent it back. The refund must be made by the same payment method unless you expressly agree otherwise, with no reimbursement fee. (Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, SI 2013/3134, reg 34)

## The 40 percent point

A deduction is possible only for diminished value resulting from handling beyond what was necessary to establish the desk’s nature, characteristics and functioning, measured by handling beyond what might reasonably be allowed in a shop. Assembly may matter if it has actually reduced resale value, but the retailer must connect the deduction to that loss. A flat 40 percent rule for all assembled goods is not supported by the supplied Regulations. If the retailer failed to give the required cancellation-right information, the handling-deduction rule would not apply at all; the facts supplied do not confirm whether that information was given. (Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, SI 2013/3134, reg 34)

# GE04 — Deduction from wages

## User question

Today is 23 September 2026. Jurisdiction: England.

I am an employee working in Manchester. My September payslip deducts £180 for a broken shop till. I did not agree to the deduction and cannot find a deduction term in my written contract. My manager says staff must cover accidents, but has not explained why I am responsible. What should I check, how should I challenge this and are there time limits? Do not assume it is a criminal accusation.

## Recorded answer

## Short answer

On the assumed facts, you have a strong basis to challenge the £180 September payslip deduction as an unauthorised deduction from wages. An employer may deduct wages only where the deduction is required or authorised by statute, authorised by a relevant written or notified contractual provision supplied before the deduction, or previously agreed by the worker in writing. You say you did not agree and cannot find a deduction term, so the manager’s general statement that staff cover accidents is unlikely to be enough unless the employer can point to a valid pre-existing written contractual basis or written consent covering this type of loss. (Employment Rights Act 1996, s 13; Acas, ‘Deductions from pay and wages’ (*Acas*) <https://www.acas.org.uk/deductions-from-pay-and-wages> accessed 23 September 2026)

## What to check

Ask payroll or management to identify the exact basis relied on: statute, a specific contract term, a written notification of an implied or express term, or your prior written consent. Check your written contract, handbook, onboarding documents, signed forms and any written policy previously given to you about damage, breakages or till losses. The responsibility point matters because, if the employer relies on a contractual damage clause, it should connect the clause to what happened and to why you are factually or contractually liable for the broken till. Common statutory exceptions such as recovery of an overpayment, court or tribunal deductions, third-party deductions, statutory disciplinary deductions, or deductions for strike action do not appear to fit these facts unless the employer identifies evidence bringing the case within one of them. (Employment Rights Act 1996, s 13; Employment Rights Act 1996, s 14; Acas, ‘Deductions from pay and wages’ (*Acas*) <https://www.acas.org.uk/deductions-from-pay-and-wages> accessed 23 September 2026)

## Retail and pay floor

Because this was in a shop, check whether the employer is treating the £180 as a retail cash shortage or stock deficiency. The statutory retail definitions concern deficits in amounts received in retail transactions and stock deficiencies arising in retail transactions, so damage to a physical till is not obviously within those definitions. If the employer says it is a till shortage or stock shortfall, Acas says deductions of that kind are allowed only if the contract permits them, are normally capped at 10% of weekly or monthly gross pay, require written notice explaining the debt and recovery method before the next payday, and must be recovered within 12 months of discovery. Also check whether the deduction took your pay below the National Minimum Wage; Acas says deductions generally must not do so unless an exception applies, including certain contractual liability for something the worker has done. (Employment Rights Act 1996, s 17; Acas, ‘Deductions from pay and wages’ (*Acas*) <https://www.acas.org.uk/deductions-from-pay-and-wages> accessed 23 September 2026)

## How to challenge

Challenge it in writing, calmly and as a wages issue. Ask for the payslip entry, the contractual or statutory authority, any written consent relied on, the calculation of £180, and the evidence said to make you responsible. Ask for repayment if no valid basis is identified. Acas suggests raising unexpected deductions informally first so the employer can explain or correct a mistake. If that does not work, raise a formal grievance. If still unresolved, you may be able to bring an employment tribunal complaint for unlawful deduction from wages. (Acas, ‘Deductions from pay and wages’ (*Acas*) <https://www.acas.org.uk/deductions-from-pay-and-wages> accessed 23 September 2026; Employment Rights Act 1996, s 23)

## Time limits

Do not wait too long. For a wages deduction complaint, the statutory tribunal limit is generally three months beginning with the date the deducted wages were paid, and Acas describes most claims as three months minus one day from the date you were not paid correctly. The exact date depends on your actual September pay date. Notifying Acas for early conciliation can pause time if done within the time limit, but internal grievance or appeal steps do not change it. Series claims may run from the last deduction, subject to the statutory limits. Acas notes that most claim limits increase from 1 October 2026 only where the time limit starts on or after that date, so that future change should not be assumed to help a September pay deduction unless your legally relevant start date is on or after 1 October 2026. (Employment Rights Act 1996, s 23; Acas, ‘Employment tribunal time limits’ (*Acas*) <https://www.acas.org.uk/employment-tribunal-time-limits> accessed 23 September 2026; Acas, ‘Deductions from pay and wages’ (*Acas*) <https://www.acas.org.uk/deductions-from-pay-and-wages> accessed 23 September 2026)

# ES01 — Consideration and estoppel

## User question

Today is 23 September 2026. Jurisdiction: England.

Write a critical essay of approximately 700 words: 'Consideration remains a necessary safeguard in English contract law, and promissory estoppel does not provide an adequate replacement.' Assess the purposes of consideration, contractual variations, promises to accept less than an existing debt, and the limits of estoppel. Develop a thesis, address counterarguments and distinguish established law from reform. Use verified authorities, OSCOLA citations and a bibliography grouped into Case law, Legislation and Secondary sources. Do not invent authorities or quotations.

## Recorded answer

## Thesis

The proposition is substantially correct, but only if stated modestly. The supplied authorities do not prove a full theory of consideration; they show why English law remains cautious before enforcing informal promises, especially variations and debt compromises. Consideration still performs a screening function because it separates enforceable contractual changes from informal accommodations. Promissory estoppel is important, but the supplied material presents it as a limited corrective against injustice, not as a general replacement for bargain-based enforceability. (*Rock Advertising Ltd v MWB Business Exchange Centres Ltd* [2018] UKSC 24 [16]; *Rock Advertising Ltd v MWB Business Exchange Centres Ltd* [2018] UKSC 24 [18])

## Purposes of consideration

The strongest supported purpose of consideration here is certainty in contractual liability. In the variation setting, the Supreme Court treated informality as capable of being restricted by the parties’ own bargain: a legal system may allow contracts to be made informally while also enforcing a clause requiring variations to be in writing. That reasoning supports the safeguard thesis because it values an objective threshold for altering obligations. The related discussion of entire agreement clauses reinforces the same concern: courts should generally give effect to agreed wording that identifies where the parties’ binding terms are to be found. (*Rock Advertising Ltd v MWB Business Exchange Centres Ltd* [2018] UKSC 24 [13]; *Rock Advertising Ltd v MWB Business Exchange Centres Ltd* [2018] UKSC 24 [14])

## Contractual variations

Contractual variations are the pressure point. Commercial parties often alter performance because both sides perceive practical value, and the supplied judgment recognises that such value may be real. Yet the established decision in the supplied material did not validate the oral variation; it gave effect to the no oral modification clause. The court also rejected the idea that parties who orally agree a substantive variation, without more, impliedly abandon the agreed formality requirement. That is central to the thesis: the law protects parties from later disputes about whether informal discussions changed the bargain, even where the alleged variation appears commercially plausible. (*Rock Advertising Ltd v MWB Business Exchange Centres Ltd* [2018] UKSC 24 [16]; *Rock Advertising Ltd v MWB Business Exchange Centres Ltd* [2018] UKSC 24 [18]; *Rock Advertising Ltd v MWB Business Exchange Centres Ltd* [2018] UKSC 24 [29])

## Promises to accept less

Promises to accept less than an existing debt expose consideration’s weakness. The supplied judgment records a tension between commercial practicality and older doctrine: an expectation of practical advantage had been treated as good consideration in one line of authority, but practical benefit was said to be inadequate in the debt context in another. The Supreme Court did not resolve the conflict. It described the issue as difficult, potentially ripe for re-examination, and inappropriate for decision where the result would be obiter. The critical point is therefore not that consideration is perfectly coherent, but that reform had not been accomplished on the supplied material. (*Rock Advertising Ltd v MWB Business Exchange Centres Ltd* [2018] UKSC 24 [18])

## Limits of estoppel

Estoppel provides the main counterweight, but the supplied authorities make its limits decisive. In the no oral modification context, the Supreme Court accepted that estoppel may prevent injustice where one party relies on the other’s conduct. But its scope cannot be so broad that it destroys the contractual certainty for which the parties stipulated. ## Interpretation

The interpretive starting point is freedom-respecting. The supplied Supreme Court reasoning rejects a rigid contest between textualism and contextualism: both are tools in the same exercise. ## Business Contracts

In business contracts, the main supplied statutory control is narrower than the consumer regime. Where one party deals on the other’s written standard terms, the other party cannot rely on a term to exclude or restrict liability for breach, or to render substantially different or no performance, unless the term satisfies reasonableness. Reasonableness asks whether inclusion of the term was fair and reasonable in light of circumstances known or reasonably contemplated when the contract was made, and the burden lies on the party relying on the term. The provision also reaches devices that make enforcement subject to restrictive or onerous conditions, so control is directed at substance as well as verbal form. ## Misrepresentation

Non-reliance clauses should be analysed carefully. The supplied materials prove that a term excluding or restricting liability for pre-contractual misrepresentation, or a remedy for it, has no effect except so far as it satisfies the statutory reasonableness requirement. They do not, without further authority, prove a complete doctrine governing every form of non-reliance clause. The defensible point is therefore narrower: English law permits contractual management of pre-contractual responsibility, but prevents drafting from automatically defeating misrepresentation liability or remedies. That supports the thesis because it protects reliance interests without abolishing commercial risk allocation. (Misrepresentation Act 1967, s 3; Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977, s 11)

## Consumer Contracts

Consumer contracts receive stronger protection. An unfair consumer term or notice is not binding on the consumer, although the consumer may rely on it. Unfairness turns on good faith, significant imbalance, and detriment to the consumer, assessed by reference to the subject matter, the circumstances when the term was agreed, and the other relevant terms. Core subject-matter and price terms may be shielded from fairness assessment, but only if transparent and prominent. Written terms and notices must be transparent, and ambiguity is resolved in the consumer’s favour. This regime treats consumer consent as less reliable where terms are hidden, unclear or imbalanced. Interpretation also remains committed to objective agreement, so it cannot by itself police every abuse of bargaining power.
