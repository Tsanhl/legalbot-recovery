## What commercial law is
- The medieval lex mercatoria (merchant custom, applied by merchant courts) was absorbed into the common law by Holt CJ and Lord Mansfield; trade usage became evidence of contractual intention. The Sale of Goods Act 1893 codified the synthesis, and s 62(2) of the 1979 Act preserves the general common law. Modern equivalents include the CISG, PECL and the UNIDROIT Principles.
- Guiding principles: commercial certainty and a transactional approach; sanctity of contract (qualified by unfair-terms and consumer-credit legislation); privity (qualified by statutory third-party rights); good faith (mainly a civil-law and US UCC concept); facilitating security interests; and ordering priorities among competing claimants through security of property (nemo dat; first in time: Phillips v Phillips (1861)), protection of good-faith purchasers (statutory nemo dat exceptions) and ostensible ownership or estoppel (s 21).

## Sources and interpretation
- SGA 1979 (consolidating the 1893 Act, amended in 1994–95), the Factors Act 1889, UCTA 1977 and the Consumer Rights Act 2015 (Part 1 Chapter 2 governs trader-to-consumer goods contracts).
- A codifying statute is read by its natural meaning without first asking what the old law was (Bank of England v Vagliano Bros [1891] AC 107). That approach is limited for implied terms, which should not force on parties obligations they could not reasonably have intended (Ashington Piggeries v Christopher Hill [1972] AC 441); "satisfactory quality" is read on its own words (Rogers v Parish [1987]).

## Key concepts
- Contract of sale of goods (ss 1, 2(1), 61): a contract by which the seller transfers or agrees to transfer property in goods to the buyer for a money consideration (the price). It covers both a sale and an agreement to sell.
- It must be a voluntary contract: statutory supply is not a sale (Pfizer v Ministry of Health [1965]: NHS prescriptions; Norweb v Dixon [1995]: regulated electricity supply).
- A sale may be absolute or conditional (s 2(3)). A conditional sale (title retained until payment) is a sale; hire-purchase is bailment plus an option to buy, with no obligation to buy, so the hirer cannot pass title (Helby v Matthews [1895]). A credit sale passes property at once with payment by instalments. A charge or mortgage over goods is outside the Act (s 2(4)) and needs a bill of sale.
