## Meaning and consequences of property
- "Property" means general property (ownership) in the goods (s 61). A sale (property passed) differs from an agreement to sell (property later: s 2(6)).
- After a sale: the buyer has rights in rem and can sue third parties; the seller can sue for the price (s 49) as well as for damages for non-acceptance (s 50); the buyer can claim the goods on the seller's insolvency; risk is on the buyer (s 20); the contract cannot be frustrated by perishing.
- Under an agreement to sell: the buyer has only personal rights and damages for non-delivery (s 51); the seller keeps responsibility and risk and can still pass good title; perishing may frustrate (s 7); on the buyer's insolvency the seller can reclaim the goods.
- Passing of property does not by itself give a right to possession against the seller: delivery and payment are concurrent conditions (s 28). A buyer who does not receive the title bargained for can recover the whole price for total failure of consideration, even after use.
- A seller left in possession can pass good title to a good-faith buyer (s 24; Pacific Motor Auctions v Motor Credits [1965]; Worcester Works Finance v Cooden Engineering [1972]: the capacity in which possession is retained is immaterial).
- Retention of title clauses that operate as charges may be void unless registered (Companies Act 2006, s 859A).
- Tort claims for damage to goods need property or possession at the time of damage (Leigh & Sillavan v Aliakmon Shipping [1986]); the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act 1992 now gives bill-of-lading holders contractual rights against carriers.

## Risk (s 20)
- Unless otherwise agreed, risk passes with property, whether or not delivered (res perit domino).
- Exceptions:
- A party at fault in delay bears losses caused by it (s 20(2); Demby Hamilton v Barden [1949]: uncollected apple juice went off).
- A party holding goods as bailee remains liable as bailee (s 20(3); Wiehe v Dennis Bros (1913)).
- Transit rules apply where the seller must send the goods (ss 32–33).
- Undivided shares in a bulk have their own rule (s 20A).
- In consumer sales, goods stay at the trader's risk until they are in the consumer's physical possession (CRA 2015, s 29).
- Risk and property can separate: under CIF contracts risk passes on shipment with insurance by the seller; under FOB, when goods pass the ship's rail. Risk can pass before property for quasi-specific goods (Sterns v Vickers [1923]). Risk can stay with the seller after property passes where the buyer has a right of rejection (Head v Tattersall (1871)). A seller sending goods at its own risk still does not bear deterioration necessarily incidental to transit (s 33), but the quality obligation continues for a reasonable time after shipment (Mash & Murrell v Joseph Emanuel [1961]).

## Goods that do not exist when the contract is made
- s 6: a contract for specific goods that, without the seller's knowledge, have perished when the contract is made is void. It is a rule of construction (Joseph Constantine SS v Imperial Smelting [1942]), and it can yield to contrary terms.
- Common-law possibilities: the buyer bought a chance (Couturier v Hastie (1856), on construction); the contract is void for common mistake (Bell v Lever Bros [1932] sets a narrow test); the seller promised existence and is liable (McRae v Commonwealth Disposals Commission (1951): no tanker ever existed, so s 6 could not apply because nothing had perished); an express condition precedent that the goods exist (Associated Japanese Bank v Credit du Nord [1989]).
- The parties may intend delivery of only what exists (Goldsborough v Carter (1900)).
- "Perish" covers commercial loss of identity, not merely physical destruction (Asfar v Blundell [1896]: contaminated dates). Rotten potatoes that are still potatoes have not perished (Horn v Minister of Food [1948]). Theft with no real prospect of recovery counts. If part of an indivisible specific lot perished, the whole contract is avoided (Barrow, Lane & Ballard v Phillips [1929]).
- Unascertained goods: purely generic goods cannot perish; the seller bears supply risk (Intertradex v Lesieur [1978]). Goods from a specified source may frustrate if the source fails (Howell v Coupland (1876); Sainsbury v Street [1972], where the seller had to offer the smaller crop actually grown).

## Frustration after contracting
- Common-law test: a supervening event, not the fault of either party, not contemplated, which radically changes the nature of the obligations and makes it unjust to hold the parties to them (National Carriers v Panalpina [1981]; British Movietonews v London and District Cinemas [1952]).
- s 7: specific goods perishing without fault after an agreement to sell, before risk passes, avoid the contract. Once risk has passed there is no frustration (Re Shipton Anderson (1915)).
- Unascertained goods are rarely frustrated: the closure of the Suez Canal did not frustrate a CIF sale (Tsakiroglou v Noblee Thorl [1962]); failure of the seller's own supplier did not (Blackburn Bobbin v Allen [1918]; CTI Group v Transclear [2008]); an export ban on a known sole source did (Re Badische (1921)). Force majeure clauses allocate such risks.
- Consequences: under s 7 cases the common law applies: money paid is recoverable only on total failure of consideration (Fibrosa v Fairbairn [1943]; formerly Chandler v Webster [1904]), and no payment for work done if payment was not yet due (Appleby v Myers (1867)). The Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943 applies to other frustrated sales (for example unascertained goods), allowing recovery of payments even on partial failure (s 1(2)) and awards for benefits conferred (s 1(3)), but it excludes s 7 cases and contracts frustrated by the perishing of specific goods (s 2(5)(c)).
