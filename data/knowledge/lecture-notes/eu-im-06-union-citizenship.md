## Background
- **Economic origins.** Free movement of persons began as an economic right for workers, the self-employed and service providers. The residence directives of 1990 loosened that link, and the Maastricht Treaty (1992) created Union citizenship.

## Key cases
- **Grzelczyk (C-184/99).** Citizenship is "destined to be the fundamental status" of Member State nationals, entitling those in the same situation to equal treatment regardless of nationality, subject to express exceptions.
- **Baumbast (C-413/99).**
- Article 21 is directly effective.
- **Rottmann (C-135/08).**
- **Ruiz Zambrano (C-34/09).**
- Article 20 precludes national measures that deprive Union citizens of the genuine enjoyment of the substance of their citizenship rights.
- **Dano (C-333/13).**
- An economically inactive citizen who is not seeking work and has no resources must meet Article 7(1)(b) of the Directive (sufficient resources and sickness insurance) to have lawful residence.
- Without lawful residence, the citizen cannot claim equal treatment in social assistance under Article 24.

## Essay theme
- Is Union citizenship now a fundamental status, or an incomplete, conditional status? It gives partial rights and imposes few duties, and later case law (Dano, Alimanovic, Commission v UK C-308/14) has re-tied equal treatment to lawful residence and economic self-sufficiency.
