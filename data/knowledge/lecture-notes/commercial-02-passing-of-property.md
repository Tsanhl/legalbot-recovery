## Why it matters
- Risk usually passes with property (s 20); the seller can sue for the price only once property has passed (s 49); property decides who wins on insolvency; it affects frustration. These rules also apply to consumer sales (CRA 2015, s 4).

## Method for problem questions
- s 16: are the goods ascertained? If not, no property can pass (subject to s 20A).
- s 17: for specific or ascertained goods, property passes when the parties intend, judged from the terms, conduct and circumstances.
- Only if intention is unclear, apply the s 18 rules.

## Section 18 rules
- "Unconditional" means no condition on which the passing of property depends (not merely unfulfilled implied terms, as Varley v Whipp [1900] seemed to suggest). Payment by cheque does not prevent passing (Dennant v Skinner and Collom [1948]: property passed at the fall of the auctioneer's hammer; a later document came too late).
- "Specific" means identified and agreed on when the contract is made (s 61). Trees meeting certain measurements were not specific (Kursell v Timber Operators [1927]); marketable timber on a site was (Joseph Reid v Schultz). Wine stock was specific only where a release covered the whole of that description in the warehouse (Customs and Excise v Everwine).
- "Deliverable state" means the buyer would be bound to take the goods (s 61(5)). An engine still bolted to the floor was not (Underwood v Burgh Castle [1922]); carpet still to be fitted was not (Head v Showfronts [1970]). Defects do not by themselves stop property passing.
- Contrary intention displaces Rule 1: express retention of title (Aluminium Industrie Vaassen v Romalpa [1976]); shop sales where property passes on agreement about payment (Ward v Bignall [1967]; supermarkets on payment: Davies v Leighton (1978); petrol on going into the tank: Edwards v Ddin [1976]); risk allocation clauses (Federspiel v Twigg [1957]; Re Anchor Line [1937]); insurance obligations as evidence; sales of land with contents; supply-and-install contracts. Merely postponing delivery or payment is not contrary intention (Clarke v Reilly (1962), criticised).
- **Rule 3**: specific goods the seller must weigh, measure or test to fix the price: property passes when done and the buyer has notice. It does not apply where the buyer is to do the act (Turley v Bates (1863); Nanka-Bruce v Commonwealth Trust [1926]).
- **Rule 4**: goods on approval or sale or return: property passes when the buyer approves or acts to adopt the transaction (for example pledging or reselling: Kirkham v Attenborough [1897]; Poole v Smith's Car Sales [1962]), or keeps them beyond the fixed or a reasonable time without notice of rejection. Any notice that the buyer does not intend to buy suffices (Atari v Electronics Boutique [1998]). Retention of property until payment overrides the rule (Weiner v Harris [1910], where the holder was an agent who could pledge).
- Delivery to a carrier, still mixed with other goods, does not appropriate (Healy v Howlett [1917]). Filling the buyer's containers appropriates (Aldridge v Johnson (1857); Langton v Higgins (1859)); notice to collect with assent inferred (Pignataro v Gilroy [1919]). Marked crates awaiting shipment did not pass where the intention was to pass on shipment (Federspiel v Twigg). A delivery order handed to a third-party warehouse appropriated goods (Wardar's v Norwood [1968]).
- Reserving a right of disposal until payment makes appropriation conditional (s 19). Ascertainment requires intention and identification (Karlshamns Oljefabriker v Eastport Navigation [1982]); ascertainment by exhaustion is recognised (Rule 5(3)).
- Undivided shares in a bulk: previously no property passed (Re Wait [1927]; Re London Wine [1986]; contrast Re Stapylton Fletcher [1994]). Since 1995, s 20A gives a buyer who has paid for a specified quantity of an identified bulk an undivided share as a tenant in common, after the Law Commission's report on goods forming part of a bulk (1993).
