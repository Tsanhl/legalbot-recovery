## Beneficiary principle
- A private trust needs someone who can enforce it and whom the court can decree performance in favour of (Morice v Bishop of Durham (1805)). A gift cannot be made to a non-charitable purpose, because a purpose cannot sue (Leahy v A-G for NSW [1959]).
- Private purpose trusts usually fail for lack of an enforcer, perpetuity or uncertainty (Re Astor's ST [1952]: press independence; Re Shaw: new alphabet; capricious purposes such as Brown v Burdett (1882)).
- Perpetuity: the 125-year period under the Perpetuities and Accumulations Act 2009 governs vesting; non-charitable purpose trusts remain subject to the rule against inalienability, so their duration must be limited.
- Anomalous valid purpose trusts (trusts of imperfect obligation), kept within narrow limits (Re Endacott [1960]): graves and monuments (Mussett v Bingle (1876); Re Hooper [1932], upheld for 21 years), specific animals (Pettingall v Pettingall (1842); Re Dean (1889); lives in being must be human lives, Re Kelly [1932]), private masses (Bourne v Keane [1919]), and fox-hunting (Re Thompson [1934], now doubtful after the Hunting Act 2004).
- Trusts that benefit identifiable people directly or indirectly can be valid (Re Denley [1969]: sports ground for employees; Re Lipinski [1977]).

## Unincorporated associations (Neville Estates v Madden [1962])
- A gift may be: charitable; a void private-purpose trust; to present members as joint tenants (each can sever, rarely what donors intend: Cocks v Manners (1871)); to members subject to the association's contractual rules (Re Recher; Re Lipinski), the usual analysis; or on trust for the association as a quasi-corporate entity, which fails unless charitable.

## Charitable trusts (Charities Act 2011)
- A charitable purpose falls within s 3(1) and is for the public benefit (s 2). Heads include relief of poverty, education, religion, health, community development, arts and heritage, amateur sport, human rights and equality, the environment, relief of those in need, animal welfare, efficiency of the emergency services, and purposes analogous to recognised charitable purposes (s 3(1)(m)).
- Public benefit has a benefit aspect and a public aspect; an object whose detriments outweigh its benefits fails (National Anti-Vivisection Society v IRC [1948]).
- Non-charitable elements invalidate unless merely ancillary; cy-près lets the court apply funds to similar purposes when the original purpose fails.
- The 2011 Act consolidated earlier statutes without changing the law.
