## Two different moments
- **At trial: unfitness to plead** (Criminal Procedure (Insanity) Act 1964, ss 4–4A).
- Test: D cannot understand the charge, cannot distinguish a plea of guilty from not guilty, cannot challenge jurors, cannot instruct counsel or cannot follow the proceedings (Pritchard (1836); Robertson [1968] 1 WLR 1767).
- **At the time of the offence: the special verdict** of not guilty by reason of insanity (Trial of Lunatics Act 1883, s 2). The same disposal options follow.

## M'Naghten Rules (1843) 10 Cl & F 200
- **Origin.** The judges' answers to questions from the House of Lords after M'Naghten's acquittal. They are not binding precedent, but have been treated as authoritative ever since.
- **Presumption of sanity.** Everyone is presumed sane. D must prove insanity on the balance of probabilities.
- **Who raises it.**
- Whether the evidence amounts to insanity is a question of law for the judge (Dickie [1984] 3 All ER 173). The judge can therefore treat a plea of automatism as insanity.
- The prosecution may raise insanity (Bratty, per Lord Denning), and may do so to rebut diminished responsibility.
- **The three elements.**
- **Disease of the mind.**
- This is a legal concept, not a medical one. It concerns the mental faculties of reason, memory and understanding, not the brain.
- It does not matter whether the condition is organic or functional, curable or not, permanent or transient (Kemp [1957] 1 QB 399; Sullivan [1984] AC 156, epilepsy).
- Lord Denning in Bratty: any mental disorder that has manifested itself in violence and is prone to recur is a disease of the mind.
- The internal/external test (Quick; Hennessy) divides insanity from automatism.
- **A defect of reason.** D's powers of reasoning must be impaired. Mere absent-mindedness or confusion is not enough; such a D simply lacks mens rea (Clarke [1972] 1 All ER 219).
- **Consequence.** As a result, D either:
- did not know the nature and quality of the act, meaning its physical character (Codere (1916) 12 Cr App R 21); or
- did not know it was wrong. "Wrong" means legally wrong (Windle [1952] 2 QB 826; Johnson [2007] EWCA Crim 1978). Knowing that the act was illegal defeats the plea even if D believed it morally right. On that reading, M'Naghten himself would have been convicted.

## Criticisms (essay material)
- **Over-inclusive:** it covers diabetics, epileptics and sleepwalkers, who would not be detained under the Mental Health Act 1983.

## Reform (Law Commission discussion paper, 2013)
- **New defence.** "Not criminally responsible by reason of a recognised medical condition". It would apply where D wholly lacked the capacity:
- to make a rational judgment about the conduct;
- to understand that it was wrong; or
- to control the physical acts.
- **Burden.** D would bear only an evidential burden, supported by two experts. The prosecution would then have to disprove the defence.
- **Automatism.** A narrowed defence would remain for a total loss of control that D was not culpable for and that no medical condition caused, such as reflexes. Diabetes, epilepsy and sleep disorders would move to the new defence.
