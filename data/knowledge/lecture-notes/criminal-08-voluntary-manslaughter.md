## Loss of control (Coroners and Justice Act 2009, ss 54–56)
- **Background.** Section 56 abolished common law provocation (formerly Homicide Act 1957, s 3). Provocation was criticised on three grounds:
- It required a "sudden and temporary" loss of self-control (Ahluwalia [1992]). This disadvantaged abused women who killed out of fear rather than in a flash of rage.
- It was unclear which characteristics the reasonable man shared.
- It had roots in sexual jealousy.
- **Procedure.**
- The judge leaves the defence to the jury only if there is sufficient evidence on which a properly directed jury could find every element (s 54(5)–(6)).
- Once the defence is raised, the prosecution must disprove it.
- **Element 1: loss of self-control (s 54(1)(a)).**
- This is subjective: did D actually lose self-control? What a reasonable person would have done is irrelevant at this stage.
- The loss need not be sudden (s 54(2); Dawes [2013] EWCA Crim 322), but a long delay makes a loss of control harder to show and a planned killing more likely (Jewell [2014] EWCA Crim 414).
- Panic or fear is not the same as a loss of self-control (Martin [2017] EWCA Crim 1359).
- A "considered desire for revenge" excludes the defence even if a trigger exists (s 54(4)).
- **Element 2: a qualifying trigger (ss 54(1)(b), 55).**
- Fear trigger (s 55(3)): D feared serious violence from V against D or another. The fear must be genuine, but it need not be reasonable or based on a correct view of the facts.
- Anger trigger (s 55(4)): things done or said that (a) amounted to circumstances of an extremely grave character and (b) caused D to have a justifiable sense of being seriously wronged. Clinton [2012] EWCA Crim 2 treats this as having two parts. Subjectively, D must feel seriously wronged. Objectively, the circumstances must be grave and the sense of grievance justifiable.
- Combination of the two (s 55(5)).
- Exclusions: a trigger D incited as an excuse to use violence (s 55(6)(a)–(b)); sexual infidelity on its own (s 55(6)(c)). Under Clinton, infidelity may still form part of the context when other triggers are present.
- **Element 3: the normal-person test (s 54(1)(c)).**
- Would a person of D's sex and age, with a normal degree of tolerance and self-restraint, in D's circumstances, have reacted in the same or a similar way?
- It is not enough that D had a good reason to lose control. The reaction must be one that anybody might have had.
- "Circumstances" means all of D's circumstances except those whose only relevance is to D's general capacity for tolerance or self-restraint (s 54(3)).
- A mental condition bearing only on that capacity is excluded from this limb (Rejmanski [2017] EWCA Crim 2061).
- Voluntary intoxication is likewise excluded where it goes only to capacity for self-control (Asmelash [2013] EWCA Crim 157).

## Diminished responsibility (Homicide Act 1957, s 2, as amended by the 2009 Act)
- **How it differs from loss of control.**
- Loss of control says a normal person might have acted as D did.
- Diminished responsibility says D should not be judged against the normal person, because a medical condition substantially impaired D.
- If the impairment is total, consider insanity instead.
- The burden of proof is on D, on the balance of probabilities.
- **The four elements.**
- An abnormality of mental functioning. The statute does not define it, so expert evidence explains the condition and how far D departs from the norm.
- The abnormality arose from a recognised medical condition, which may be physical, psychological or psychiatric. Courts are guided by the ICD and DSM classifications and by reliable expert evidence.
- It substantially impaired D's ability to understand the nature of their conduct, to form a rational judgment or to exercise self-control (s 2(1A)).
- "Substantial" means significant or weighty: less than total but more than trivial (Golds [2016] UKSC 61; Squelch [2017] EWCA Crim 204).
- It provides an explanation for the killing, meaning it caused, or was a significant contributory factor in causing, D's conduct (s 2(1B)). It need not be the sole cause; intoxication may coexist with it (Dietschmann [2003] UKHL 10).

## Suicide pact (Homicide Act 1957, s 4)
- D must have agreed with V that both would die, and at the time of killing D must have intended to die in pursuance of the agreement (s 4(3)).
- The burden of proof is on D.
