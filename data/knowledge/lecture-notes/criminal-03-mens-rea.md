# Mens rea
- **Intention**: judges should usually not elaborate (Moloney [1985]).
- Oblique intention: where the result is a virtual certainty (barring unforeseen intervention) and D appreciated that, the jury may find intention (Woollin [1999] AC 82); they are entitled but not obliged to (Matthews and Alleyne [2003]).
- **Knowledge**: true belief; belief that something may be so is not enough (Saik [2006] UKHL 18; Montila [2004]).
- **Recklessness** (R v G [2003] UKHL 50, returning to Cunningham [1957]): D is aware of a risk that the circumstance exists or the result will occur, and it is unreasonable in the circumstances known to D to take it. Any level of foreseen risk suffices (Brady [2006]); it does not matter that anger drove the risk from D's mind (Parker [1977]; Stephenson [1979]). "Maliciously" and "wilfully" in older statutes mean reckless (Cunningham; Sheppard [1981]; A-G's Ref (No 3 of 2003) [2004]).
- **Strict liability**: there is a presumption that mens rea is required (Sweet v Parsley [1970] AC 132), rebuttable by statutory language, due-diligence defences, regulatory purpose and the like (Warner [1969]). Common for driving and regulatory offences.
- **Mistake**: an honest mistake about an element can negate mens rea even if unreasonable (DPP v Morgan [1976]), subject to statutory exceptions such as the Sexual Offences Act 2003 reasonableness test. Mistake of law is no excuse.
- **Transferred malice**: mens rea transfers to an unintended victim of the same offence (Latimer (1886)) but not across different offences (Pembliton (1874); A-G's Ref (No 3 of 1994) [1998]).
- **Constructive liability**: some offences require mens rea only for a lesser element (s 47 ABH needs only the mens rea of assault or battery).
- **Coincidence**: actus reus and mens rea must coincide, but a series of acts can be treated as one transaction (Thabo Meli [1954], a pre-planned killing; Church [1966], no plan; Le Brun [1992]: murder if D had the intent at the initial blow).
