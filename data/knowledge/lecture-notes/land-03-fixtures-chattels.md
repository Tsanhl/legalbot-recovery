# Fixtures and chattels
- Three categories: chattels (moveable, not part of the land); fixtures (chattels attached so as to become part of the land); and items "part and parcel" of the land itself, such as a bungalow or structural elements (Elitestone v Morris [1997]).
- Why it matters: fixtures pass with the land on sale (LPA 1925, s 62), are subject to a mortgage, and belong to the landlord at the end of a lease; chattels do not.
- Test (Holland v Hodgson (1872); Elitestone): two factors, the degree of annexation and, now more important, the purpose of annexation.
- Degree: items resting by their own weight are presumed chattels unless shown to be intended as part of the land; attached items are presumed fixtures. The modern emphasis is on whether removal would require demolition or cause serious damage (Berkley v Poulett [1977]; Elitestone).
- Purpose: attached temporarily for the better enjoyment of the object itself means a chattel; attached to improve the land permanently means a fixture (Hellawell v Eastwood (1851)). Items forming part of an architectural design can be fixtures even if free-standing (D'Eyncourt v Gregory (1866); contrast the paintings and statue in Berkley v Poulett and the tapestries in Re Whaley).
- Illustrative cases: Dibble v Moore [1970] (greenhouses); Mew v Tristmire [2011] (houseboat on a platform); Chelsea Yacht & Boat v Pope [2000]; Tower Hamlets v Bromley [2015] (sculpture on a plinth); Royal Parks v Bluebird Boats [2021] (boathouse on a slab); Botham v TSB [1996] (everyday household items).
