## Stage 1: establishing the equity (Taylors Fashions v Liverpool Victoria Trustees [1982])
- Assurance (express, or passive through silence or acquiescence) that the claimant has or will have rights in the land. It must be clear enough in context (Thorner v Major [2009]; Thompson v Thompson [2018]; Habberfield v Habberfield [2019]). Commercial parties negotiating "subject to contract" usually cannot rely on it (Yeoman's Row v Cobbe [2008]). Vague promises of future financial security or of a job do not suffice (Mate v Mate [2023]; Gladstone v White [2023]). A testamentary-style promise can found estoppel despite being revocable in form (Gillett v Holt [2001]).
- Reasonable reliance, judged objectively (James v James [2018]). Once assurance and detriment are shown, reliance is presumed (Greasley v Cooke [1980]); the approach is flexible (Wayling v Jones (1993)).
- Detriment: improving the owner's land, financial loss, or non-financial sacrifices, judged in the round.
- Unconscionability, which runs through all the elements: the court weighs benefits received against detriment, and both parties' conduct.

## Stage 2: satisfying the equity
- Historically "the minimum equity to do justice" (Crabb v Arun DC [1976]); then proportionality between expectation and detriment (Jennings v Rice [2002]: quasi-bargain cases lean to expectation, others to a proportionate award). Examples: Davies v Davies [2016] (award reduced to £500,000); Habberfield (expectation-based award of about £1.2m upheld).
- Guest v Guest [2022] UKSC 27 (3:2):
- Majority (Lord Briggs): the starting point is the expectation, the aim is to remove unconscionability, and proportionality is a cross-check against an award out of all proportion. Accelerating a future benefit (for example an inheritance received early) calls for a discount. The parents could choose between putting the farm on trust (with the son's 40% taking effect after their life interests) and a discounted immediate payment.
- Third parties: in registered land an estoppel equity is proprietary from creation (LRA 2002, s 116) and binds purchasers if protected by notice or overriding through actual occupation; in unregistered land the doctrine of notice applies (ER Ives Investment v High [1967]).
