## Four groups of rights
- Interests that must be completed by registration to be legal (registrable dispositions, s 27).
- Unregistered interests that override (Sch 1 on first registration; Sch 3 on later dispositions).

## Titles and registration
- First registration is voluntary (s 3) or compulsory (s 4); the main triggers are a transfer of unregistered freehold, a transfer of an unregistered lease with more than 7 years to run, and the grant of a lease of more than 7 years out of an unregistered title.
- Registration is conclusive and constitutive (s 58): title by registration. Classes of title: absolute, qualified, possessory, good leasehold.
- Registrable dispositions (s 27; completion requirements in Sch 2) operate at law only once registered; otherwise at best in equity. They include transfers, leases over 7 years, express legal easements (s 27(2)(d)) and legal charges.
- A registered proprietor has full owner's powers (ss 23–24); a disponee need not look behind the register for limits not shown on it (s 26).

## Priority
- Basic rule (s 28): earlier interests prevail over later ones.
- The exception that usually applies (s 29): a registrable disposition of a registered estate, for valuable consideration, completed by registration, takes priority over any earlier interest unless it is protected by a registered charge or a notice, overrides under Sch 3, or is excepted from registration. If any element is missing, fall back to s 28. Grants of short leases that are not registrable dispositions are treated as registered at the time of grant (s 29(4)).
- Notices (ss 32–34) protect most third-party interests (not, for example, trust interests: s 33). Restrictions limit dealings; the usual one protects beneficial interests under a trust by requiring overreaching.

## Overreaching
- Detaches overreachable equitable interests (chiefly beneficial interests under a trust) from the land into the purchase money (LPA 1925, s 2), promoting free alienability at the cost of occupiers' security.
- Conditions: a conveyance of a legal estate (including a mortgage or lease: s 205(1)(ii)) and capital money paid to at least two trustees or a trust corporation (ss 2, 27). It operates without the beneficiary's consent (City of London BS v Flegg [1988]: the occupiers got nothing).
- Not overreachable: equitable leases, equitable easements, estate contracts (s 2(3)); these depend on ss 28–29.
- No overreaching where money is paid to a single trustee (Williams & Glyn's Bank v Boland [1981]), or in some fraud cases (HSBC v Dyche [2009]). Granting an easement is not a conveyance for this purpose (Baker v Craggs [2018]). Where no capital money arises, payment to two trustees is unnecessary (State Bank of India v Sood [1997]; Shami v Shami [2012]).
- If overreaching fails, apply ss 28–29 and consider whether an overriding interest exists.
