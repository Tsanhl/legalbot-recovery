## Status and review
- The settlement comes into existence after the mediation, not in the course of it (State Bank of NSW v Freeman (NSW)).
- A confidentiality clause in an agreement to mediate does not bar evidence of mediation communications offered to prove the settlement's terms, unless the clause clearly says so (Union Carbide Canada v Bombardier 2014 SCC 35).

## Vitiating factors (rarely successful)
- **Undue influence.** Studer v Konig (NSW) failed because the other party did not know of the solicitor's alleged misconduct and the claimant had affirmed by signing.
- Relevant questions: did the party protest, have alternatives, or have advice? Commercial pressure alone is not duress (Abriel v Westpac (FCA): a claim was not struck out).
- In the US, Olam v Congress Mortgage enforced the settlement despite the claimant's account. The mediator's testimony was admitted.
- **Unconscionability.** It requires the stronger party's knowledge of the weaker party's disability (Pittorino v Meynert).
- Crystal Decisions v Vedatech and Gilford v Burdon both failed.

## Converting and enforcing
- Enforceable under the New York Convention, which the prevailing view accepts.
- **UNCITRAL Model Law on International Commercial Mediation (2018).**
- Article 15: a settlement agreement is binding and enforceable.
- Section 3 (Articles 16–20) mirrors Singapore Convention Articles 1–6: scope, general principles, requirements for reliance, refusal grounds and parallel claims. It is deliberately detailed; enacting states should avoid creative changes.
- Unlike the Convention, it covers all settlement agreements. It serves as an implementing framework and as an alternative for states not ready to ratify.
- **Singapore Convention on Mediation (2019).**
- refusal grounds under Article 5, including incapacity, invalidity, non-final or modified settlements, serious breach of mediator standards, non-disclosure by the mediator, and public policy;
- reservations under Article 8.
