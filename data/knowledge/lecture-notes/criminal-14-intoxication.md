## Core principles
- **A denial of mens rea, not a defence.** Intoxication neither excuses nor justifies. It is at most evidence that D lacked mens rea, and it can make an otherwise implausible claim of no mens rea more credible (DPP v Beard [1920] AC 479).
- **A drunken intent is still intent.** Drunken awareness of a risk is still recklessness (Sheehan and Moore [1975] 1 WLR 739). It does not matter that D would not have acted when sober.
- **Kingston [1995] 2 AC 355.** D's drink was spiked to lower his inhibitions, and he then indecently assaulted a boy. He had the intent, so he was guilty. Involuntary intoxication went only to sentence.

## Involuntary intoxication
- Spiking. D's mistake about the strength of a drink D knowingly took does not make it involuntary (Allen [1988] Crim LR 698).
- A non-dangerous (sedative) drug, unless D was subjectively reckless in taking it (Hardie [1985] 1 WLR 64: Valium).

## Voluntary intoxication: the Majewski rules (DPP v Majewski [1977] AC 443)
- **Specific intent offences** (for example murder, s 18, theft, robbery). Voluntary intoxication can be relied on to show that D did not in fact form the intent.
- **Basic intent offences** (recklessness suffices: battery, assault, ABH, s 20, criminal damage, manslaughter). Voluntary intoxication with alcohol or dangerous drugs is no answer, however extreme.
- On Majewski's reasoning, becoming intoxicated is itself a reckless course of conduct that supplies the fault.
- **Classification.**
- There is no single logical test; classification is largely policy, decided offence by offence (Heard [2007] EWCA Crim 125).
- Heard held sexual assault (Sexual Offences Act 2003, s 3) to be basic intent even though it cannot be committed recklessly. Specific intent covers offences requiring proof of purpose or a consequence, including ulterior intent.
- A practical approach is to treat an offence as one of specific intent where the courts have allowed intoxication evidence to count (Smith, Hogan and Ormerod).
- The Law Commission (Law Com No 229, 1995; Law Com No 314, 2009) criticised the uncertainty and proposed replacing the labels with categories of fault.
- **Theories of the rule:**
- a substantive rule (prior fault replaces the mens rea for the offence);
- an evidential rule (ask whether D would have had mens rea if sober);
- pure policy;
- moral equivalence between becoming drunk and acting recklessly.

## Interaction with other doctrines
- **Mistaken beliefs.** A drunken mistaken belief in the need for self-defence cannot be relied on (Criminal Justice and Immigration Act 2008, s 76(5); O'Grady; Hatton). The same applies to a drunken belief in consent for sexual offences.
- **Other conditions.** Psychosis caused by alcohol withdrawal was not treated as intoxication (Harris [2013] EWCA Crim 223). Contrast Taj [2018], where psychosis linked to earlier drug use was governed by the intoxication rules.
