## Definition (s 1(1))
- A person is guilty of theft if they dishonestly appropriate property belonging to another with the intention of permanently depriving the other of it.
- Analyse five elements in order. The actus reus is appropriation, property and belonging to another. The mens rea is dishonesty and intention to permanently deprive.

## Appropriation (s 3)
- **Definition.**
- Any assumption of the rights of an owner is an appropriation.
- This includes a later assumption by keeping or dealing with property that D first came by innocently.
- **Assuming any one right is enough** (Morris [1984] AC 320; Gomez [1993] AC 442). Swapping price labels is an example.
- **Consent is irrelevant.**
- An act authorised by the owner can still be an appropriation (Lawrence v MPC [1972] AC 626; Gomez). Gomez rejected Morris's "adverse interference" gloss.
- Even receiving a valid gift can be an appropriation, leaving dishonesty to do the work (Hinks [2001] 2 AC 241). This is a frequent essay target because of the conflict with civil law.
- **Timing.** Theft can occur in an instant or through a course of dealing involving several appropriations (Atakpu [1994] QB 69).

## Property (s 4)
- **What counts.** Money and all other property, real or personal, including things in action and other intangible property.
- **Limits:**
- land, which generally cannot be stolen except as s 4(2) provides;
- wild plants, unless picked for sale or reward (s 4(3));
- wild creatures, unless tamed or reduced into possession (s 4(4)).
- **Not property:**
- electricity (Low v Blease (1975); there is a separate offence in s 13);
- confidential information (Oxford v Moss (1979) 68 Cr App R 183);
- corpses, although body parts become property if work or skill has been applied to them (Kelly [1999] QB 621).

## Belonging to another (s 5)
- **Basic rule.** Anyone with possession or control, or any proprietary right or interest, counts as an owner.
- **Owners can steal.** An owner can steal from a person in possession, such as a garage holding a car for repairs (Turner (No 2) [1971] 1 WLR 901).
- **Civil law governs** who has a proprietary interest (Marshall [1998] 2 Cr App R 282, resale of used Tube tickets).
- **Abandoned property** does not belong to another, but true abandonment is rare. Goods left outside a charity shop were not abandoned (R (Ricketts) v Basildon Magistrates' Court [2010] EWHC 2358 (Admin)).
- See also s 5(3) (property received for a particular purpose) and s 5(4) (property got by mistake).

## Dishonesty (s 2 and Ivey)
- **Section 2(1): D is not dishonest if D believes:**
- (a) that D has a legal right to deprive;
- (b) that the owner would consent if they knew; or
- (c) that the owner cannot be found by taking reasonable steps (not for trustees or personal representatives).
- **Otherwise, the Ivey test** (Ivey v Genting Casinos [2017] UKSC 67; adopted for criminal law in Barton and Booth [2020] EWCA Crim 575) applies:
- What was D's actual state of knowledge or belief as to the facts? This is subjective. Reasonableness is evidence of whether D genuinely held the belief, not a requirement.
- On those facts, was the conduct dishonest by the objective standards of ordinary decent people?
- **Ghosh replaced.** The second, subjective limb of Ghosh [1982] QB 1053 (did D realise that ordinary people would regard it as dishonest?) no longer applies.

## Intention to permanently deprive (s 6)
- **Treating the thing as one's own.** D is treated as intending permanent deprivation if D intends to treat the thing as D's own to dispose of regardless of the owner's rights.
- **Borrowing** counts only where it is for a period and in circumstances equivalent to an outright taking. Taking a season ticket and returning it after its value is exhausted is an example (Lloyd [1985] QB 829).
- **Conditional parting** also counts: pawning property under a condition as to its return that D may not be able to perform (s 6(2)).

## Worked pattern
- **Dishonesty.** Consider s 2(1)(b): did D believe the owner would consent, given the earlier permission?
- **Intention to permanently deprive.** Using it for one fixture and returning it does not exhaust its value, so under s 6 there is probably no intention to permanently deprive.
