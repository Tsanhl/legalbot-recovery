## Defamation Act 2013, s 9
- **The rule.** For defendants not domiciled in the UK, the court has no jurisdiction unless, "of all the places in which the statement complained of has been published", England and Wales is clearly the most appropriate place to sue. The statement includes substantially the same imputation (s 9(3)).
- **Effects.**
- Worldwide publication must now be weighed.
- As a jurisdiction rule, it cannot be waived by submission. If the claim fails, the claimant must start again abroad and risks limitation.
- **Concerns.**
- Does "appropriateness" include the fairness and access-to-justice limb (Spiliada limb 2)?
- If a publication is equal in England and the US, a deserving claimant may be shut out.
- **What remains.**
- Claims can still be brought against defendants served in England (presence, or a place of business) and against UK-domiciled defendants.
- Service-out cases can proceed where England is clearly appropriate, and may include global damage.
- The prospect of English law applying continues to attract claimants.
- **Soriano v Forensic News LLC [2021] EWCA Civ 1952.**
- Facts: a UK resident with British and Israeli citizenship sued a Californian outlet and US journalists over a podcast and articles. Permission to serve out was given for libel and some misuse of private information claims.
- Warby LJ held that s 9 modifies the Spiliada test rather than removing subject-matter jurisdiction. It makes two changes: all places of publication must be considered, and substantially similar statements are included.
- The claimant bears the legal burden. A defendant challenging jurisdiction bears an evidential burden to identify an available alternative forum and prove the facts showing it is at least as suitable.
- The standard is a good arguable case, meaning the better of the argument. Foreign law on the alternative forum need not always be proved by expert evidence.
- The claimant's reputation was centred in England and he sued only for English harm, so he was "not a libel tourist". The defendants had not shown whether California would entertain a claim for English reputational harm.
- Critique: Dickinson, "A boost for libel tourism" (2022) 138 LQR 357.

## Choice of law in defamation
- **Phillips v Eyre (1870) LR 6 QB 1.**
- **Boys v Chaplin [1971] AC 356.** A Malta road accident between English servicemen. As an exception, English law alone applied. This recognised a third idea, tort as loss distribution between the parties, influenced by US doctrine.
- **Red Sea Insurance v Bouygues [1995] 1 AC 190 (PC).** The law of the place of the tort (Saudi Arabia) could apply exclusively where it had the most significant relationship with the claim. This moves the rule towards conduct regulation by the lex loci.
- Church of Scientology v Commissioner of Police (1976): a report sent from England to Germany was published in Germany.
- Chadha v Dow Jones [1999] ILPr 829 and Berezovsky v Michaels (Forbes) [2000] UKHL 25: small English circulation of US magazines was a distinct English publication governed solely by English law.
- The tort occurs where the material is downloaded (compare Metall und Rohstoff v DLJ [1990] QB 391 on locating torts). English downloads mean English law.

## Online and social media challenges
- Website operators have a defence under Defamation Act 2013, s 5, unless they fail to identify the poster or remove the material after notice.
