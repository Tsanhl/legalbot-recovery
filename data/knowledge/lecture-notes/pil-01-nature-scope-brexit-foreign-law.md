## English courts as a forum
- **Forum shopping.** In The Atlantic Star (1972) the Court of Appeal called England "a good place to shop in, both for the quality of the goods and the speed of the service" (Lord Denning MR).
- **The House of Lords' response** ([1974] AC 436).
- **Competition between courts.** The Singapore International Commercial Court (2015) is modelled on the Commercial Court. London has responded with specialist lists such as the Financial List.

## European influence and Brexit
- **Before Brexit.** EU instruments governed most jurisdiction and judgments questions (the Brussels regime) and choice of law (Rome I and II, maintenance, insolvency), through the European Communities Act 1972. The ECHR bears on PIL through the Human Rights Act 1998.
- **Lugano.** The UK applied to rejoin Lugano independently, but the EU refused consent (June 2021).
- **Rome I and II** stay in force as assimilated law. They do not depend on reciprocity (Retained EU Law (Revocation and Reform) Act 2023, s 5).
- Choice of Court 2005, in force for the UK without interruption from 1 October 2015 and given domestic force by the Private International Law (Implementation of Agreements) Act 2020;
- The UK has since joined the Hague Judgments Convention 2019, in force for the UK from 1 July 2025. Verify current status.

## Case study on a human rights claim (Song Mao v Tate & Lyle)
- **Facts.** Cambodian villagers sued English-domiciled companies in conversion. They alleged that sugar made from cane grown on land taken from them was converted when delivered in London.
- **Jurisdiction.** English domicile gave jurisdiction (Brussels I Article 2 then; service within the jurisdiction at common law). Claimants said Cambodian courts lacked independence. The case raised hard questions of foreign land title.
- **Choice of law.**
- Characterisation is difficult. Is conversion a tort under Rome II, a tort under the Private International Law (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1995, or a property issue under the common law?
- Each route probably leads to English law, the place of interference.
- The incidental question of the claimants' title to the sugar goes to the lex situs at the time of acquisition, which is Cambodian law and must be proved.
- **Outcome.** The case left the listings, probably through a confidential settlement. This illustrates how private litigation limits public information.

## Foreign law in English courts
- It must be pleaded and proved (Mostyn v Fabrigas (1774)). It is "a question of fact of a peculiar kind" (Parkasho v Singh [1968] P 233).
- **Presumption of similarity.** If foreign law is pleaded but not proved, it is generally presumed to be the same as English law. Brownlie v FS Cairo [2021] UKSC 45 explained this as a flexible presumption, less apt where the foreign law is likely to differ.
- **Difficulty of proof.** Neilson v Overseas Projects Corp of Victoria [2005] HCA 54 shows the difficulty of proving how a foreign court (there, Chinese) would apply its rules.

## Substance and procedure
- limitation: substantive (Foreign Limitation Periods Act 1984);
- damages: substantive under Rome I and II. In areas outside Rome I and II, such as defamation, the assessment of damages is procedural (Harding v Wealands [2006] UKHL 32). How loss is proved, including what expert evidence is needed, is procedural even under Rome II (Wall v Mutuelle de Poitiers [2014] EWCA Civ 138).
