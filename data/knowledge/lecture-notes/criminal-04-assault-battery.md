# Assault and battery
- Rationale: bodily inviolability; the law forbids even the least touching (Collins v Wilcock [1984]). The OAPA 1861 is a "rag-bag" (Professor Smith), but the offences should be read as a coherent ladder (Ireland; Burstow [1998] AC 147).
- Both are summary offences under the Criminal Justice Act 1988, s 39 (maximum six months), defined by case law (Fagan v MPC [1969]; Ireland). "Common assault" is used loosely for both. They are base offences for aggravated offences (s 47, assaulting a constable).
- No injury, violence or hostility is needed (Faulkner v Talbot [1981]; Afolabi [2017]), and no foresight of injury (Logdon v DPP [1976]).

## Assault
- Words, letters, messages and even silent calls can suffice (Constanza [1997]; Ireland).
- Words can negate an assault ("If it were not assize-time, I would not take such language": Tuberville v Savage (1669)).
- Immediacy: V must expect force then and there (Lamb [1967]), though fear of violence "at any time, not excluding the immediate future" can suffice (Constanza); V being safely out of reach can negate it, but not if V is terrified (Smith v Chief Superintendent, Woking (1983)).
- Mens rea: intention or subjective recklessness as to V's apprehension (Venna [1976]; Savage and Parmenter [1992]).

## Battery
- Actus reus: the infliction of unlawful force, meaning any unlawful touching including clothes (Thomas (1985)). V need not be aware. It may be by an instrument, a car wheel (Fagan) or a set-on dog (Murgatroyd v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire [2000]); indirectly (Haystead v Chief Constable of Derbyshire [2000]: punching a mother who dropped her baby; DPP v K [1990]: acid in a hand dryer); or by omission after creating a danger (DPP v Santana-Bermudez [2003]: needle in a pocket after assuring the officer there was none).
- Mens rea: intention or recklessness as to unlawful contact (Venna). A genuine though unreasonable belief in facts making the force lawful negates unlawfulness (Williams (Gladstone) (1984)).
