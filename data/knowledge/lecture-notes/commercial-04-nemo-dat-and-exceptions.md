# Nemo dat and the exceptions
- Rule: a seller who is not the owner, and lacks the owner's authority or consent, passes no better title than it has (SGA 1979, s 21(1)). Two principles compete: protecting property and protecting good-faith commercial purchasers (Bishopsgate Motor Finance v Transport Brakes [1949], Lord Denning). A third-party buyer wins only by fitting an exception. Encumbrances, such as a pledgee's rights over goods released to the pledgor for a limited purpose, can also bind a later buyer.

## Exceptions
- **Authority or consent** (agency); a sub-sale assented to by the unpaid seller defeats its lien and stoppage (s 47(1)).
- **Estoppel** ("unless the owner is by his conduct precluded from denying the seller's authority": s 21(1)). The representation must be of fact, unambiguous, voluntary and acted on to the buyer's detriment (duress excluded: Debs v Sibec Developments [1990]).
- By words: Henderson v Williams [1895]; Eastern Distributors v Goldring [1957], which can pass real title.
- By conduct: merely letting another possess goods or documents is not enough (Farquharson Bros v King [1902]; Mercantile Bank of India v Central Bank of India [1938]; a car's registration document is not a document of title: Central Newbury Car Auctions v Unity Finance [1957]). There is no estoppel where the buyer knew the seller was only an agent (Lloyds & Scottish Finance v Williamson [1965]).
- By negligence: requires a duty of care owed to the buyer; carelessness with one's own property is not enough (Moorgate Mercantile v Twitchings [1977], failure to register a hire-purchase agreement; Mercantile Credit v Hamblin [1965]). Silence acquires positive content only where there is a duty to speak (Lord Wilberforce).
- Shaw v Metropolitan Police Commissioner [1987]: s 21 was held to apply to buyers who had only agreed to buy; the better reason the claim failed was that no price had been paid, so there was no detriment.
- **Special powers of sale** (s 21(2)(b)): a pledgee's power of sale, sales by enforcement officers, and court-ordered sales (for example of perishables under the CPR).
- **Mercantile agents** (Factors Act 1889, s 2(1)): a mercantile agent in possession of goods or documents of title with the owner's consent, disposing of them in the ordinary course of business of a mercantile agent to a person in good faith without notice, passes title.
- A mercantile agent has, in the customary course of business, authority to sell or pledge goods for others (s 1(1); a dealer who happened to hold jewellery for others qualified in Weiner v Harris [1910]; contrast Lowther v Harris [1927]).
- The agent must be in possession at the time of the disposition (Beverley Acceptances v Oakley [1982]).
- Documents of title are defined widely (s 1(4)), but a car logbook is not one.
- Consent must be to possession in the agent's capacity as agent (a repairer who is also a dealer does not qualify: Pearson v Rose & Young [1951]); withdrawal of consent does not affect a buyer without notice (s 2(2)); consent is presumed (s 2(4)).
- Ordinary course of business means acting as a mercantile agent normally would (Oppenheimer v Attenborough [1908]); selling a car without its registration document was not in the ordinary course (Stadium Finance v Robbins [1962]; Pearson).
- Suspicious circumstances, such as a price far below value or an "open" cheque, defeat good faith (Heap v Motorists' Advisory Agency [1923]).
- **Sale under voidable title** (s 23): a buyer in good faith without notice gets good title if the seller's voidable title had not been avoided at the time of sale. A contract void for mistake of identity gives no title (Cundy v Lindsay (1878); Shogun Finance v Hudson [2003] UKHL 62: written dealings versus face-to-face dealings, where identity mistakes usually make the contract only voidable, Phillips v Brooks [1919]). Rescission can be effected by conduct, such as telling the police, where the rogue cannot be found (Car & Universal Finance v Caldwell [1965]).
- **Seller in possession** (s 24; Factors Act, s 8): a seller who continues in (physical) possession after sale and delivers the goods or documents to a good-faith second buyer passes title, whatever capacity it holds in (Pacific Motor Auctions v Motor Credits [1965]; Worcester Works Finance v Cooden Engineering [1972]). A substantial break in possession defeats the exception (Mitchell v Jones (1905)). There is authority requiring physical delivery to the second buyer (Nicholson v Harper [1895]), doubted where constructive delivery occurred (Michael Gerson Leasing v Wilkinson [2001]).
- **Buyer in possession** (s 25; Factors Act, s 9): a person who has bought or agreed to buy and obtained possession with the seller's consent can pass title to a good-faith sub-buyer on delivery or transfer. A hire-purchaser has not agreed to buy (Helby v Matthews [1895]); a conditional buyer has (Lee v Butler [1893]), except a consumer-credit conditional sale (s 25(2)). Constructive delivery to the first buyer can suffice (Four Point Garage v Carter [1985]); temporary loan possession counts (Marten v Whale [1917]); delivery must be voluntary (Forsythe v Silver Shipping [1994]). The disposition is judged as if by a mercantile agent in the ordinary course (Newtons of Wembley v Williams [1965], a street-market car sale); not without a logbook (Lambert v G & C Finance (1963)). The "owner" whose consent matters is not a thief's victim (National Employers Mutual v Jones [1990]).
- **Motor vehicles on hire-purchase** (Hire Purchase Act 1964, Part III, s 27): a debtor under a hire-purchase or conditional sale agreement who disposes of the vehicle to a private purchaser in good faith without notice passes the creditor's title. It does not apply if the agreement is void (Shogun Finance v Hudson). Dealers and finance companies are not private purchasers (s 29(2); GE Capital Bank v Rushton [2005]; Stevenson v Beverley Bentinck [1976]). Knowing of hire-purchase is not notice if the purchaser believes it was paid off (Barker v Bell [1971]).
