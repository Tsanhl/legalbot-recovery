## Capability (Re Ellenborough Park [1956])
- A dominant and a servient tenement, both identifiable (London & Blenheim Estates v Ladbroke Retail Parks [1994]).
- Different ownership or at least occupation (no easement over one's own land: Roe v Siddons (1888); a tenant may hold one over the landlord's land: Wright v Macadam [1949]).
- The right must accommodate the dominant land: benefit its normal enjoyment, not merely give a personal or commercial advantage (Hill v Tupper (1863)); the plots must be reasonably close. Purely recreational rights can now qualify if all elements are met (Regency Villas v Diamond Resorts [2018] UKSC 57, 4:1, leisure facilities for timeshare owners; Lord Carnwath dissented, calling it a new property right akin to club membership).
- The right must be capable of forming the subject matter of a grant: a capable grantor and grantee, sufficiently certain, no positive obligation on the servient owner (Liverpool CC v Irwin [1977]), within or analogous to recognised categories, and not amounting to possession.
- Ouster principle: a right that leaves the servient owner no reasonable use of the land is not an easement (Batchelor v Marlow [2003]: parking six cars all working day failed). Parking can be an easement (Virdi v Chana [2008]; Kettel v Bloomfold [2012]); storing coal in a shed passed (Wright v Macadam); exclusive use of a cellar (Grigsby v Melville [1974]) and parking cars awaiting repair (Copeland v Greenhalf [1952]) failed. The Law Commission recommends abolishing ouster.

## Creation
- Express grant or reservation. A legal easement must be equivalent to a fee simple or term of years, made by deed (LPA 1925, s 52) and, in registered land, completed by registration. Otherwise it is equitable (for example for life, or by specifically enforceable contract).
- Necessity: without it the land cannot be used at all (Union Lighterage v London Graving Dock [1902]; Manjang v Drammeh (1990); Re MRA Engineering (1987); a more lenient view of residential vehicle access in Sweet v Sommer [2004]).
- Common intention: a shared intention for a definite and particular use, which requires the easement (Pwllbach Colliery v Woodman [1915]; Wong v Beaumont Property Trust [1965]; Stafford v Lee (1992)).
- Wheeldon v Burrows (1879): on sale or lease of part, quasi-easements the seller used become easements if continuous and apparent (Ward v Kirkland [1967]) and reasonably necessary for enjoyment (Wheeler v JJ Saunders [1996]).
- LPA 1925, s 62: a conveyance can upgrade rights, even permissions, into easements (Wright v Macadam); the right must be capable of being an easement and exercised at the time; where there was no prior diversity of occupation, it must be continuous and apparent (Wood v Waddington [2015]).
- Termination: unity of ownership of both plots, release or abandonment, obsolescence, estoppel; excessive use can be restrained (McAdams Homes v Robinson [2004]).

## Third parties
- The benefit passes with the land (s 62).
- Registered land: express legal easements are registrable dispositions; implied legal easements can be noted or override under Sch 3 para 3; equitable easements need a notice.
- Law Commission Report 327 (2011) proposed reforms, including a single test for implication.
