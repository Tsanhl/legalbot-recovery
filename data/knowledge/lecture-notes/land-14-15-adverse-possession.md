# Adverse possession
- A squatter who takes ordinary possession without the owner's consent for the required period can acquire title (J A Pye (Oxford) v Graham [2002] UKHL 30; Rains v Buxton (1880)).
- Arguments for: rewarding use and improvement, housing need, certainty of title and utility. Arguments against: it resembles land theft, rewards wrongdoing, and engages the owner's rights under ECHR A1P1. Squatting in residential buildings is now a criminal offence, which affects claims (R (Best) v Chief Land Registrar [2015] EWCA Civ 17).

## Elements (cumulative)
- Factual possession: an appropriate degree of single and exclusive physical control, dealing with the land as an occupying owner would (Powell v McFarlane (1977)). What suffices depends on the land's nature and ordinary use; acts on part may evidence possession of the whole; enclosure is strong evidence. No hostility is needed (Pye), but fraud or deliberate concealment is not allowed.
- The period: 12 years for unregistered land (Limitation Act 1980, s 15), and for registered land where 12 years were completed before 13 October 2003 (LRA 1925, s 75). Under the LRA 2002, 10 years.
- Time stops running if the owner starts proceedings, retakes possession (Zarb v Parry [2011] EWCA Civ 1306), the squatter acknowledges the owner's title in writing, or the squatter leaves.

## LRA 2002 procedure (Sch 6)
- After 10 years the squatter applies to the registry.
- The registry notifies the owner and other interested parties (procedural cases include Baxter v Mannion [2011]; Fontaine v Bortolussi; Hopkins v Beacon; Franks v Chief Land Registrar).
- If the owner serves a counter-notice, the application fails unless the squatter proves estoppel (para 5(2)), some other entitlement (para 5(3)), or a reasonable mistake as to boundary (para 5(4): adjoining land, boundary not determined, reasonable belief for 10 years, land registered for over a year).
- If rejected, the owner has two years to evict.
- If the owner does not act, a renewed application succeeds.
- This makes success on registered land much harder despite the shorter period.

## Human rights
- Pye v UK (Grand Chamber, 2007): adverse possession under the LRA 1925 did not violate A1P1. Earlier decisions went both ways (Beaulane Properties v Palmer [2005]; Chamber judgment 2005); later confirmed in Ofulue v Bossert [2008].
