## Personal scope (Article 3)
- **Who is covered.** Union citizens who move to or reside in a Member State other than their own (the host state), and their family members of any nationality.
- **Nationality of another Member State counts.** Chen (C-200/02): a child born in Northern Ireland acquired Irish nationality and so was a Union citizen. With sufficient resources and insurance, the child could reside in the UK, and her primary-carer mother derived a right to reside with her. The UK could not question the effect of Irish nationality law.
- **Core family members (Article 2(2)):**
- the spouse (Metock (C-127/08): no requirement of prior lawful residence in another Member State);
- a registered partner, where the host state treats registered partnership as equivalent to marriage;
- direct descendants under 21 or dependent;
- dependent direct relatives in the ascending line;
- in each case, including those of the spouse or partner.
- Dependency is a factual, individual assessment. The need for material support must exist in the state of origin, and it can be proved by any appropriate means (Jia, C-1/05).
- **Extended family (Article 3(2)).** The host state must "facilitate" entry and residence for:
- other dependants or household members;
- relatives who need personal care on serious health grounds;
- a durable partner.
- This is a discretionary right requiring an extensive examination of personal circumstances.
- **Returning citizens.** Where a citizen created or strengthened family life with a third-country national during genuine residence in another Member State, the Directive applies by analogy on return home, through Article 21 TFEU (O and B, C-456/12; see also Singh, C-370/90).
- **Dual nationals.** A citizen who naturalises in the host state falls outside the Directive but can rely on Article 21 TFEU for a third-country spouse (Lounes, C-165/16).

## Residence tiers
- **Exit and entry (Articles 4–5).** A passport or ID card suffices. Third-country family members may need a visa, and Article 5(4) requires a chance to prove their right.
- **Up to three months (Article 6).** No conditions, but the host state need not provide social assistance (Article 24(2)). The right lasts as long as the person is not an unreasonable burden (Article 14(1)).
- **More than three months (Article 7).** One of the following:
- worker or self-employed, with status retained in the Article 7(3) cases;
- job-seeker with evidence of continuing to seek work and a genuine chance of being engaged (Article 14(4)(b));
- student: enrolled, insured, and declaring sufficient resources. Family rights are narrower for students (Article 7(4)).
- **Permanent residence (Article 16).**
- It is lost only after more than two consecutive years' absence (Article 16(4)).
- Workers may qualify earlier (Article 17).
- It brings full equal treatment, frees the holder from the Article 7 conditions, and gives stronger protection against expulsion.

## Equal treatment (Article 24)
- **Article 24(1).** Equal treatment with host-state nationals.
- **Article 24(2) exceptions.**
- No duty to provide social assistance in the first three months, or for longer to first-time job-seekers.
- No maintenance aid for studies before permanent residence.
- Neither exception applies to workers, the self-employed and their families (García-Nieto, C-299/14).
- **Between three months and five years.** Equal treatment in social assistance depends on lawful residence under Article 7 (Dano, C-333/13).
