## Context (useful for essays)
- **Rape myths.** Empirical work (Temkin and Krahé, 2008) found that jurors attribute less blame to D the closer the relationship. "Female precipitation" beliefs can override evidence of force or of stated non-consent.

## Rape (s 1)
- **Actus reus:** penile penetration of the vagina, anus or mouth, without consent. Absence of consent is part of the actus reus.
- **Mens rea:** intentional penetration, and no reasonable belief in consent. Whether a belief is reasonable is judged having regard to all the circumstances, including steps D took to ascertain consent (s 1(2)).

## Three routes to consent, applied in this order
- If the prosecution proves the relevant act and a s 76 circumstance (and, where required, D's knowledge), non-consent and lack of belief in consent are conclusively presumed.
- s 76(2)(a): D intentionally deceived V as to the nature or purpose of the act.
- This is read narrowly. The old cases involved deception about the nature of the act itself, such as pretended surgery (Flattery (1877)) or pretended voice therapy (Williams [1923]).
- Jheeta [2007] EWCA Crim 1699: lies designed to pressure V into sex did not deceive V as to its nature or purpose, but they could go to s 74.
- s 76(2)(b): D induced consent by impersonating someone personally known to V (compare Elbekkay [1995]). Lies about D's own attributes, such as being a doctor, are not covered (Richardson [1999] QB 444, a non-sexual case).
- The courts are reluctant to use s 76 because its effect is conclusive. They prefer s 74 for deception (Assange v Swedish Prosecution Authority [2011] EWHC 2849 (Admin): s 76 is not exhaustive).
- The prosecution proves the act, a s 75(2) circumstance and D's knowledge of that circumstance. D must then raise sufficient evidence to rebut non-consent, reasonable belief, or both.
- The circumstances in s 75(2):
- Rebuttal evidence must be more than speculative (Ciccarelli [2011] EWCA Crim 2665). Cases where the presumption stands unrebutted are relatively rare (White [2010] EWCA Crim 1929).
- The more pressure on V, the less freedom. The effect of long-term abuse and control can be decisive (C [2012] EWCA Crim 2034; Kirk [2008] EWCA Crim 434).
- Every consent involves submission, but mere submission is not consent (Olugboja [1982] QB 320).
- In an abusive relationship the jury may need a direction distinguishing reluctant but free consent from submission out of fear (Doyle [2010] EWCA Crim 119).
- Grooming can reduce consent to mere acquiescence (Ali [2015] EWCA Crim 1279).
- agreement to withdraw before ejaculation (R (F) v DPP [2013] EWHC 945 (Admin));
- D's gender (McNally [2013] EWCA Crim 1051).
- fertility, including a lie about a vasectomy (Lawrance [2020] EWCA Crim 971);
- an undercover officer's identity or occupation (R (Monica) v DPP [2018] EWHC 3508 (Admin));
- HIV status for the purposes of consent to the sexual act (B [2006] EWCA Crim 2945; this is separate from liability for transmission under the Offences Against the Person Act 1861).
- Capacity is assessed at the time of the act, in relation to this act with this person. V must be able to understand the relevant information and weigh it to reach a choice (Cooper [2009] UKHL 42).
- **Intoxication.** A drunken consent is still consent, but capacity may evaporate well before unconsciousness. This is fact-specific (Bree [2007] EWCA Crim 804; jury guidance in Kamki [2013] EWCA Crim 2335).
- The prosecution need not prove that V's non-consent was communicated to D, and V may be unable to remember (H [2007] EWCA Crim 2056).

## Answer technique
- In a problem question, consider s 76, then s 75, then s 74, starting with the most serious presumption the facts support.
