## Definition
- An involuntary movement of the body after a complete destruction of voluntary control (Watmore v Jenkins [1962] 2 QB 572).
- An act done by the muscles without control by the mind, such as a spasm, reflex or convulsion, or an act done while unconscious, for example through concussion (Bratty v A-G for Northern Ireland [1963] AC 386, per Lord Denning).
- D bears an evidential burden, which normally requires medical evidence. D's own assertion that D "blacked out" is rarely enough (Bratty; Dervish [1968] Crim LR 37).

## The three elements
- Reduced or impaired control is not enough, and there is no defence of irresistible impulse (A-G for South Australia v Brown [1960] AC 432).
- Drivers who could still steer or react were not automatons: Watmore v Jenkins; Broome v Perkins [1987] Crim LR 271; A-G's Ref (No 2 of 1992) [1994] QB 91, a case of "driving without awareness". See also Coley [2013] EWCA Crim 223.
- **An external cause.** An internal cause means insanity (Bratty; Sullivan [1984] AC 156).
- Hypoglycaemia caused by insulin plus a failure to eat has an external cause, so it is automatism (Quick [1973] QB 910).
- Hyperglycaemia caused by not taking insulin has the disease as its cause, so it is insanity. Stress and anxiety were treated as a state of mind prone to recur, not as external causes (Hennessy [1989] 2 All ER 9).
- Ordinary stresses and disappointments of life are internal. In Rabey (Canada, 1980) a romantic rejection was treated as internal.
- A dissociative state caused by PTSD after rape was external (T [1990] Crim LR 256).
- **Sleepwalking.** It is treated as internal, so insanity (Burgess [1991] 2 QB 92), despite older dicta favouring an acquittal.
- D cannot rely on a condition that D induced or could reasonably have foreseen. For example, a driver who feels drowsy must stop (Kay v Butterworth (1945) 173 LT 191; Quick).

## Self-induced automatism
- **Specific intent offences.** Automatism can always negate the mens rea (Bailey [1982] 1 WLR 760).
