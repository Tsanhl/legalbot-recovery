# Consent
- ABH and above: consent is effective only if the conduct also falls within a recognised exception (R v Brown [1994] 1 AC 212: consensual sado-masochistic injuries were criminal; the minority would not have criminalised private consensual conduct without public harm; A-G's Ref (No 6 of 1980) [1981]: consensual fighting in the street is unlawful).
- Implied consent covers the ordinary contacts of everyday life, unless persistence goes beyond generally acceptable standards (Collins v Wilcock [1984]; Wood v DPP [2008]).
- capacity, now assessed functionally under the Mental Capacity Act 2005 (historically by status: Burrell v Harmer [1967]);
- information, so V must know the relevant risk (Konzani [2005] EWCA Crim 706);
- no fraud as to identity (compare Richardson [1999], where deception about professional status did not vitiate, with Melin [2019] EWCA Crim 557) or as to the nature of the act, not merely collateral matters (Mobilio [1991]);
- no duress actually causing the apparent consent (Jones (1986)).
- tattooing, piercing, haircutting and ritual circumcision; branding a spouse at her request was treated like tattooing (Wilson [1996]), but body modification such as tongue-splitting or removing an ear is not an exception (BM [2018] EWCA Crim 560);
- lawful sports played within the rules or with reasonably expected foul play; a criminal prosecution is reserved for play so far outside the rules as not to be consented to, judged by the type and level of sport, the nature of the act, the degree of force, the risk of injury and D's state of mind (Barnes [2005] EWCA Crim 3246);
- rough horseplay, where even an unreasonable genuine belief in consent suffices (Jones [1987]; Aitken [1992]), but not where belief in consent was incredible (A [2005]);
- informed consent to the risk of sexually transmitted infection (Dica [2004]; Konzani).
- Critique: the line is drawn inconsistently (Brown compared with Wilson; Emmett (1999): setting fire to a partner's breasts was not protected). The reasoning reflects legal moralism and paternalism; feminist critiques warn that "consent" may mask domestic abuse.
- Domestic Abuse Act 2021, s 71: consent to serious harm (ABH, wounding or GBH) for sexual gratification is no defence, except for informed consent to the risk of sexually transmitted infection.
- Reasonable chastisement cannot justify ABH or worse against a child (Children Act 2004, s 58).
- Necessity can justify acting without consent to save someone (Re F [1990]).
