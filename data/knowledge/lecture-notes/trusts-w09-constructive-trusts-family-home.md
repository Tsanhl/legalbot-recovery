## Implied trusts
- Implied trusts are not declared, need no writing (LPA 1925, s 53(2)) and cannot "fail", because a court finds them. Roughly: a constructive trust makes an interest move where there was a good reason for it to move but an obstacle stopped it; a resulting trust returns an interest that was never meant to move. Equity abhors a beneficial vacuum (Vandervell v IRC [1967]).
- Examples of "carrying property over": Re Rose [1952] (everything in the donor's power done); Pennington v Waine [2002] (almost everything done and revocation unconscionable).

## Family homes
- Legal title can be held only as joint tenants (LPA 1925, ss 1(6), 36(2)); equitable title may be joint tenancy (four unities, survivorship, no shares) or tenancy in common (undivided shares that pass by will or intestacy).
- An express declaration of beneficial shares is conclusive absent fraud or mistake (Goodman v Gallant [1986]), whatever later contributions are made (Clarke v Harlowe [2005]). Declarations are often missing: the TR1 box is optional, conveyancers miss it, buyers misunderstand joint tenancy. Formality matters for land (Shirt v Shirt [2012]); compare personal property such as a boat, where an informal oral trust sufficed (Rowe v Prance [1999]).
- Married couples' property on divorce is now handled by statutory reallocation, emphasising sharing (White v White [2001]); unmarried cohabitants rely on trusts law.
- **Joint legal owners:** start from equal beneficial shares; departure is exceptional and needs an actual, inferred or (at the quantification stage) imputed intention, judged on the whole course of dealing (Stack v Dowden [2007] UKHL 17: 65/35 split; Jones v Kernott [2011] UKSC 53: intentions can change over time, 90/10). Method: first ask whether the parties intended unequal shares (fairness cannot be imputed at this stage); then quantify by actual agreement, inferred intention, or failing that the share the court considers fair (Oxley v Hiscock [2004]). The presumption of equality does not apply to investment purchases between family members (Laskar v Laskar [2008]; contrast Marr v Collie [2017]). Lord Neuberger's dissent in Stack favoured contributions.
- a purchase-money resulting trust (direct contemporaneous contribution to price: Dyer v Dyer (1788)); or
- a common intention constructive trust (Lloyds Bank v Rosset [1991] 1 AC 107): express common intention from discussions, however imprecise, or common intention inferred from conduct, plus detrimental reliance. "Excuse" cases count as express intention (Eves v Eves [1975]; Grant v Edwards [1986]). Detriment must be conduct not otherwise explicable than by expecting an interest. Indirect contributions may now support an inference (Le Foe v Le Foe [2001]; Abbott v Abbott [2007]), though James v Thomas [2007] set a higher bar. Quantification is broad-brush on the whole course of dealing (Midland Bank v Cooke [1995]).
- Constructive trusts are now preferred over resulting trusts in domestic cases (Abbott v Abbott [2007]).
