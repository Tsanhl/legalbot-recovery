## Certainty of intention
- The question is objective: did the settlor, in substance, intend to create a trust? No special words (not even "trust") are needed (Re Kayford Ltd [1975]). A settlor who enters an arrangement that has the effect of a trust need not realise it is one (Twinsectra v Yardley [2002] 2 AC 164, Lord Millett).
- Informal words can suffice (Paul v Constance [1976]: "the money is as much yours as mine").
- The obligation must be legally binding rather than left to the recipient's conscience (McCormick v Grogan (1869)).
- Precatory words such as "in full confidence" are neutral; context decides. In Re Adams and the Kensington Vestry no trust arose; in Comiskey v Bowring-Hanbury [1905] AC 84 the wider context showed a trust.
- Writing: a declaration of trust of land must be evidenced in signed writing (LPA 1925, s 53(1)(b)).

## Certainty of subject matter
- The trust property must be identified. Unidentified goods from a larger stock cannot be held on trust until they are separated (Re London Wine Co [1986]); intangible identical assets such as shares raise a harder question.
- Vague descriptions fail conceptually ("the bulk of my estate", Palmer v Simmonds (1854)); but an objective yardstick can save a gift ("reasonable income", Re Golay's WT [1965]). A trust of the residue is certain because the residue is ascertainable.
- Each beneficiary's share must also be certain (Boyce v Boyce (1849): the gift failed when the person who was to choose died before choosing).

## Certainty of objects
- Discretionary trusts (trustees must distribute but choose among a class) and powers of appointment (the holder may choose whether to appoint) use the "is or is not" test: can it be said of any given person whether they are or are not in the class? (McPhail v Doulton [1971]; Re Gulbenkian's ST [1970] for powers). Lord Wilberforce reasoned that a discretionary trustee of a large class never needs a complete list.
