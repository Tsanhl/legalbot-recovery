# Implied terms: title and description
- Classification matters: breach of a condition allows rejection and termination; breach of a warranty gives damages only (s 11(3)); innominate terms depend on the consequences. In non-consumer sales, a breach of ss 13–15 so slight that rejection would be unreasonable is treated as a breach of warranty (s 15A).

## Title (s 12)
- Condition that the seller has the right to sell (s 12(1)), including under an agreement to sell at the time property is to pass. A seller may perform without ever holding title by procuring a third party to pass it (The Elafi [1982]). Even an owner breaches if the sale could be restrained, for example for trade-mark infringement (Niblett v Confectioners' Materials [1921]).
- Effect of breach: total failure of consideration allows recovery of the full price despite use (Rowland v Divall [1923]); even a later perfection of title does not cure it (Butterworth v Kingsway Motors [1954]).
- Warranties of freedom from undisclosed charges and of quiet possession (s 12(2)). Quiet possession protects against the seller's own wrongful disturbance (The Playa Larga [1983]; a time-lock installed on a computer: Rubicon Computer Systems v United Paints [2000]) and lawful disturbance by third parties with superior rights, even arising after sale (Microbeads v Vinhurst Road Markings [1975]: a later-granted patent), but not wrongful acts of strangers. It overlaps with s 12(1) (Mason v Burningham [1949]).
- A seller may sell only such limited title as it has, if that intention appears (s 12(3)–(5)).
- Consumer contracts: CRA 2015, s 17 gives equivalent terms; they cannot be excluded (s 31). SGA s 12 does not apply to those contracts (s 12(7)).

## Description (s 13)
- In a sale by description, goods must correspond with the description (a condition: s 13(1A)).
- Sales by description include future and unascertained goods and specific goods sold as goods of a kind (Grant v Australian Knitting Mills [1936]), including goods selected in self-service (s 13(3)) or chosen from exhibition examples (Scancom v RWH Enterprises).
- The words must be intended as a term and relied on: expressions of opinion do not count (Drake v Agnew (1854)); a dealer buying from a self-confessed non-expert did not rely on the attribution of a painting (Harlingdon & Leinster v Christopher Hull Fine Art [1991]).
- The words must identify the goods (a "substantial ingredient" of identity), not their quality: "fair average quality" was a quality term (Ashington Piggeries v Christopher Hill [1972]). Older strict cases (Re Moore & Landauer [1921]: tins packed in 24s instead of 30s) are now seen as excessively technical (Reardon Smith Line v Hansen-Tangen [1976]).
- CRA 2015: goods must be as described (s 11); digital content must match its description, including any trial version (s 36); this also applies when content is transmitted, streamed, or later updated (ss 39–40); goods supplied by reference to a model must match it except for differences drawn to the consumer's attention (s 14).
