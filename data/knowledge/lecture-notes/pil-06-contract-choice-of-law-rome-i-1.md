## From the proper law to Rome I
- **History.** The governing law moved from the place of contracting, to the place of performance, to party choice (Vita Food Products v Unus Shipping [1939] AC 277).
- **Rome Convention 1980.** Applied to contracts made after 1 April 1991 (Contracts (Applicable Law) Act 1990). It was a common law and civil law compromise, explained by the Giuliano–Lagarde Report.
- **Rome I Regulation (EC) 593/2008.** Applies to contracts concluded after 17 December 2009. The retained version applies to contracts from 1 January 2021 (SI 2019/834) and is substantively very similar.
- Courts may now depart from EU case law (European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018, s 6, as amended in 2023), but will seldom do so in commercial matters, where stability is essential.

## Scope of Rome I
- **Subject matter (Article 1(1)).**
- Restitution after a void contract falls within Rome I (Article 12(1)(e)).
- **Universal application (Article 2).** A law specified by Rome I applies even if it is the law of a non-EU state.
- **Internal UK conflicts.** Article 22(2) does not require Rome I for conflicts between UK jurisdictions, but the UK applies it anyway (SI 2009/3064), except Article 7 on insurance.

- **Evidence and procedure** are excluded (Article 1(3)), subject to Article 18: the governing law's presumptions and burden of proof apply.

## Express choice (Article 3(1))
- **Existence and validity.** Whether a choice exists, and whether it is valid, is governed by the putatively chosen law (Articles 3(5) and 10(1)).
- **Interpretation.** Whether a clause amounts to a choice is a matter of interpretation. It should be approached through the Regulation, not narrow national rules of construction, to avoid circularity with Article 12(1)(a).

## Implied choice
- **The test.** A choice must be made expressly or "clearly demonstrated" by the terms of the contract or the circumstances (Rome Convention: "demonstrated with reasonable certainty"). The change aligned the language versions and has no substantive effect.
- **Giuliano–Lagarde.** There must be a real choice not expressly stated. The court may not infer a choice the parties had no clear intention of making; that case goes to Article 4. A standard form known to be governed by a particular law, such as the Lloyd's marine policy, is an example of a real choice.
- **Amin Rasheed Shipping v Kuwait Insurance [1984] AC 50.**
- Facts: a Liberian and Dubai assured insured a vessel with a Kuwaiti insurer, on the Lloyd's SG form scheduled to the Marine Insurance Act 1906, with no express choice of law. The vessel was seized by Saudi authorities for alleged smuggling.
- Debate: does needing English law to interpret a contract mean it is governed by English law? Compare Bingham LJ in The Komninos S [1991] 1 Lloyd's Rep 370: no rule of law dictates the inference about intention.
- Compagnie Tunisienne v Compagnie d'Armement [1971] AC 572: a London arbitration clause is not conclusive but can support an inference.
- Egon Oldendorff v Libera Corp [1996] 1 Lloyd's Rep 380 (Clarke J):
- the approach is purposive and Convention-based, and a real choice with clear intention is required;

## Absence of choice (Article 4)
- **Rome Convention, Article 4.**
- The governing law was the law of closest connection.
- This was presumed to be the law of the residence or place of business of the characteristic performer, with special presumptions for immovables and carriage.
- Article 4(5) displaced the presumptions if the contract was more closely connected elsewhere.
- **Rome I, Article 4.**
- (1) Fixed rules for particular contracts:
- sale of goods: the seller's habitual residence;
- services: the provider's;
- rights in rem in, or tenancies of, immovables: the situs, with a short private tenancy exception going to the landlord's residence;
- franchise: the franchisee's;
- distribution: the distributor's;
- auction: the place of auction;
- multilateral trading systems: the system's law.
- (2) Otherwise, or where a contract falls within several categories, the habitual residence of the characteristic performer.
- (3) An escape clause where the contract is "manifestly more closely connected" with another country.
- (4) Closest connection where the law cannot otherwise be determined.
- The "manifestly" test makes the escape narrower than under the Convention.
- **Characteristic performance.** The obligation that marks the nature of the contract and links it to its socio-economic setting. Usually it is the non-monetary performance for which payment is made: the contract's "centre of gravity".
- **Samcrete Egypt v Land Rover Exports [2001] EWCA Civ 2019.**
- Facts: an Egyptian shareholder guaranteed its Egyptian affiliate distributor's debts to Land Rover. Samcrete had deleted the English law and jurisdiction clause, apparently without Land Rover noticing.
- Under Article 4(2), the characteristic performance was payment by the guarantor, so the presumption pointed to Egypt.
- Held: the presumption should be displaced only where connecting factors clearly justify it. It is not merely a tie-breaker, but some discretion is needed across contract types. Compare the Dutch Supreme Court's strict approach in Société Nouvelle des Papeteries de l'Aa (1992).
- It was displaced here. Payment was to be made in England, so any breach and cause of action arose there. The consideration (Land Rover's supplies) was delivered ex UK works in sterling, and the distribution agreement chose English law. The contract's centre of gravity was in England.
- Question: would Rome I's "manifestly more closely connected" test change the result?
