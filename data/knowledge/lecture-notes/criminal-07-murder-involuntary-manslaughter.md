## Murder (common law; Coke's definition, now dated)
- Actus reus: unlawfully causing the death of a human being under the King's peace. The year-and-a-day rule was abolished (Law Reform (Year and a Day Rule) Act 1996).
- A human being is one fully born with an existence independent of the mother (Poulton (1832); Enoch (1833); the cord need not be cut: Reeves (1839)). Killing a foetus that is later born alive and dies is not murder (A-G's Ref (No 3 of 1994) [1998]: no double transfer of malice), but may be manslaughter.
- Death is defined medically (brain-stem death: Airedale NHS Trust v Bland [1993]).
- A British national's murder abroad can be tried here (OAPA 1861, s 9).
- Mens rea ("malice aforethought"): intention to kill or to cause GBH, whether direct or oblique. Intention is not premeditation, which matters only to sentencing (Sentencing Act 2020, Sch 21). Malice transfers to an unintended victim of the same offence (Latimer).

## Unlawful (and dangerous) act manslaughter (constructive)
- D committed a criminal base offence, with all its elements and no defence (Lamb [1967]). It must be an act, not an omission (Lowe [1973]); not an offence of negligence (Andrews v DPP [1937]) or, logically, strict liability; V need not be the target (Watson [1989]). Common bases: assaults, one-punch deaths and pranks, criminal damage by fire (Farnon [2015]), burglary (Watson), people-smuggling (Nica [2021]).
- The act was objectively dangerous: a sober and reasonable person would see the risk of some physical harm (Church [1966]; DPP v Newbury [1977]), not merely emotional harm (Dhaliwal [2006]). The reasonable person has the knowledge D had or should have had during the act (Dawson (1985); Watson: a frail elderly occupier).
- The act caused death, with no mens rea as to death needed. The chain is broken by the victim's free, deliberate and informed self-injection of drugs (Kennedy (No 2) [2007]); fleeing in fright may not break it (Lewis [2010]).

## Gross negligence manslaughter (Adomako [1995] 1 AC 171; Rose [2017] EWCA Crim 1168)
- A duty of care owed to V, interpreted broadly (doctor and patient; driver and passenger: Barker [2003]; employer and employee: Dean [2002]; people-smugglers: Wacker [2003]; a duty arising from creating a danger: Evans [2009], who supplied heroin to her half-sister and then failed to get help).
- Breach, judged by what a reasonable person would have done.
- A serious and obvious risk of death.
- The risk was reasonably foreseeable at the time of breach, not with hindsight (Singh [1999]; Misra [2004]; Rose [2017]).
- Causation, including for omissions (would reasonable action have prevented the death?).
- The negligence was so bad as to be criminal, "truly exceptionally bad" (Sellu [2016] EWCA Crim 1716), a jury question aided by experts.
