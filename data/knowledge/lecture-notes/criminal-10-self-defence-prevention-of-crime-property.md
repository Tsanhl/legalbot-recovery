## Sources and structure
- Prevention of crime is a public defence under the Criminal Law Act 1967, s 3.
- Self-defence and defence of property are private defences at common law. The Criminal Justice and Immigration Act 2008, s 76 now clarifies how all of them apply, so start with the statute.

## Exclusions
- Forceful conduct against property counts, such as breaking a door (Swales v Cox [1981] QB 849).
- Conduct without a violent or percussive character does not (Blake [1993] Crim LR 586).
- An initial aggressor or provoker may still rely on self-defence if V's response escalates (Rashford [2005] EWCA Crim 3377; Keane [2010] EWCA Crim 2514). The key question is whether D's own response was disproportionate.
- The defence is lost where D engineered the situation so that force would become "necessary" (Mason (1756); Browne [1973] NI 96).

## Element 1: was force necessary?
- **Purpose.** D must use force in order to defend or prevent crime. Retaliation or revenge for an attack that is over is excluded (Williams [2020] EWCA Crim 193).
- Subjective stage: take the facts as D honestly believed them to be, even if the belief was mistaken or unreasonable (s 76(3)–(4); Yaman [2012] EWCA Crim 1075). The jury asks whether the belief was genuine, not whether it was reasonable.
- There is no duty to retreat. A chance to retreat is one factor for the jury (s 76(6A); Field [1972] Crim LR 435).
- Immediacy is more flexible for prevention of crime (Kelly [1989] NI 341). This point is debated in cases of abused women who kill.
- **Innocent parties.** Proportionate force may be used against an innocent third party where that is the only way to prevent a crime by another person (Hichens [2011] EWCA Crim 1626).

## Element 2: was the degree of force reasonable?
- **The test.** Judged on the facts as D believed them to be, was the level of force objectively reasonable (s 76(1)(b), (3))?
- **Force, not consequences.** The jury assesses the force used, not the harm that happened to result (Keane; Noye [2011] EWCA Crim 650).
- D "may not be able to weigh to a nicety the exact measure" of the force needed (s 76(7)(a)).
- Evidence that D did only what they honestly and instinctively thought necessary is strong evidence of reasonableness (s 76(7)(b)).
- Avoid hindsight (Reed v Wastie [1972] Crim LR 221).
- **Disproportionate force** is unreasonable in non-householder cases (s 76(6)).

## Special categories of mistaken belief
- **Voluntary intoxication.** A mistake caused by voluntary intoxication cannot be relied on (s 76(5)).
- Martin (Anthony) [2001] EWCA Crim 2245 held that a psychiatric condition making D perceive a greater threat was not relevant to whether the force was reasonable.
- Press [2013] EWCA Crim 1849 allowed a genuine PTSD-driven belief to be considered when assessing what D believed.
- Taj [2018] EWCA Crim 1743 treated a psychotic belief linked to earlier drug use as governed by the intoxication rule. The reasoning has been criticised as strained.

## Defence of property
- **Basis.** A long-standing common law right, going back to the principle that one's house is one's castle and to Green v Goddard (1704).

## Householder cases (s 76(5A), (8A)–(8F), added by Crime and Courts Act 2013, s 43)
- **Conditions.**
- D is not a trespasser.
- D acts in self-defence (not prevention of crime under s 3) while in, or partly in, a building that is D's dwelling or forces accommodation.
- D believes V is a trespasser. The belief need not be reasonable, and V's status can change during a visit (Cheesman [2019] EWCA Crim 149).
- **Degree of force.**
- Grossly disproportionate force is never reasonable.
- Force that is disproportionate but not grossly so is not automatically reasonable. The jury must still decide whether it was reasonable in all the circumstances as D believed them to be.
- Authority: R (Collins) v Secretary of State for Justice [2016] EWHC 33 (Admin), which rejected an Article 2 ECHR challenge on this reading, and Ray [2017] EWCA Crim 1391.
