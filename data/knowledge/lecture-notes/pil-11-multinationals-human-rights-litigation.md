## Vedanta Resources v Lungowe [2019] UKSC 20
- **Facts.**
- 1,826 poor Zambian villagers alleged toxic discharges from the Nchanga copper mine from 2005.
- The defendants were Vedanta, the UK holding company, which publicly claimed control over the mine operator KCM as if wholly owned, and KCM, the Zambian operator in which the Zambian state held a minority stake.
- The claims were negligence and breach of Zambian statutory duty.
- **Jurisdiction.**
- Over Vedanta: its UK domicile under Brussels I Recast, Article 4.
- Over KCM: service out through the "necessary or proper party" gateway (PD 6B para 3.1(3)).
- **Held:**
- **No abuse of EU law.** Article 4 allows any claimant to sue a UK-domiciled defendant regardless of other connections. The genuine purpose was to obtain damages from Vedanta, even though attracting jurisdiction over KCM was a contributing motive. Abuse exceptions are narrow.
- **A real triable issue against Vedanta.**
- The test is summary judgment, without cross-examination or disclosure, and must be applied proportionately.
- The question is whether the parent itself intervened in the management of the relevant operations enough to owe a duty of care, or to be liable under Zambian statute.
- The parent–subsidiary relationship shows only the opportunity to do so ([49]).
- There is no special or novel category of parent liability, and no rule that group-wide policies can never create a duty.
- A duty can arise where the parent issues group-wide policies and actively implements them through training, supervision and enforcement, or where it holds itself out as exercising supervision and then fails to do so ([51]–[53]).
- The claim was arguable.
- **England was not the proper place.**
- The judge erred in treating the risk of irreconcilable judgments as decisive. Vedanta had offered to submit to Zambia, so that risk was the claimants' own choice.
- Looked at in the round, Zambia was plainly the natural forum: Zambian claimants, a Zambian mine, Zambian law, and Zambian judgments are enforceable in England.
- **But there was a real risk that substantial justice could not be obtained in Zambia.**
- Group actions were practically impossible to fund: no legal aid, and conditional fees unlawful.
- Zambia lacked legal teams with the size and experience for litigation of this scale against a well-resourced opponent.
- This was established by cogent evidence, without impugning the independence of Zambian judges. The claim stayed in England.
- **Proportionality warning ([6]–[14]).** Jurisdiction fights must be proportionate. Echoing Lord Templeman in Spiliada, arguments should take hours, not days, and appeals should be rare. Substantial-justice issues deserve anxious scrutiny because of comity, but that is no excuse for disproportion elsewhere ([11]).

## Okpabi v Royal Dutch Shell [2021] UKSC 3
- **Facts.** About 42,000 Nigerian claimants in the Ogale and Bille communities alleged oil spills from pipelines operated by SPDC, a Nigerian subsidiary operating under a joint venture. They sued the UK parent RDS as the anchor defendant, alleging that it controlled or assumed responsibility for SPDC's operations.
- **Below.** The High Court and the Court of Appeal (majority, Sales LJ dissenting) found no arguable duty of care, so the claim against SPDC failed as well.
- **Supreme Court: appeal allowed.**
- The Court of Appeal ran a "mini-trial", weighing evidence instead of asking whether the pleaded case was arguable. Pleaded facts should be accepted unless demonstrably untrue or unsupportable.
- Further errors:
- treating group-wide policies as never capable of founding a duty, contrary to Vedanta;
- focusing on control rather than on whether the parent took over or shared management of the relevant activity;
- treating parent liability as a distinct category of negligence.
- Real triable issues existed on the pleadings, two internal RDS documents and the prospect of further disclosure. Shell's vertical structure, with delegation of authority over safety and the environment, raised questions about how it worked in practice.
- **Aftermath.**
- Jurisdiction was later agreed.
- The claims now include fundamental rights claims under the Nigerian Constitution and the African Charter, added in 2023, all governed by Nigerian law, which is a common law system.
- Twenty-two preliminary issues of Nigerian law are being tried, with the main trial listed for 2027 (Alame v Shell [2023] EWHC 2961 (KB); [2024] EWCA Civ 1500; [2025] EWHC 1539 (KB)).

## Jurisdiction before and after Brexit
- The parent was sued as of right under Brussels I Article 4, and Owusu v Jackson (C-281/02) barred a forum non conveniens stay.
- No English case of this kind has yet failed on forum grounds, thanks to limb 2 access to justice (Connelly v RTZ [1998] AC 854 (Namibia); Lubbe v Cape [2000] 1 WLR 1545 (South Africa); Vedanta).

## Limbu v Dyson Technology [2024] EWCA Civ 1564
- **Facts.** 24 Nepalese and Bangladeshi migrant workers in Malaysian supplier factories alleged trafficking, forced labour and abuse. They sued two English Dyson companies (served as of right) and a Malaysian company (served out) in negligence and unjust enrichment. The judge stayed the claims in favour of Malaysia.
- **The Court of Appeal reversed:**
- The same Spiliada question applies to service in and service out: the forum for the interests of all the parties and the ends of justice, with the most real and substantial connection, subject to the substantial justice limb.
- The English domicile of D1 and D2 and their service as of right weighed for England. D3's Malaysian domicile was not an equal and opposite weight, because the English companies were the main protagonists.
- The defence would be coordinated from England by English officers wherever the trial took place. That is a significant connecting factor.
- The judge overlooked other England-located factors relevant to duty, breach, harm and remedy. The centre of gravity was England, or at least neutral.
- Dyson's own overlapping English defamation claim against broadcasters (later discontinued) was relevant, as the position at the hearing is what counts.
- The defendants' unprecedented undertakings to fund the claimants' evidence did not cover all disbursements.
- Equality of arms: the claimants were impoverished and vulnerable, the defendants well-resourced, and the allegations serious human rights abuses. Top-tier representation for Dyson against lesser representation for the claimants in Malaysia favoured England.

## Choice of law
- **Scope.** Rome II excludes company law matters (Article 1(2)(d)), but a company's capacity to incur tort liability falls within Rome II (recital 12; Article 15(a), which covers who may be held liable).
- **General rule.** Article 4(1) applies the law of the country where the damage occurs, usually the host state.
- **Environmental damage (Article 7).** The claimant may choose the law of the country where the event giving rise to the damage occurred. That could point to the parent's home state if its decisions there count as the event.
- **Article 17.** Rules of safety and conduct at the place and time of the event are taken into account as a matter of fact.
