## Hin-Pro v CSAV continued
- **Contract interpretation.** Arnold v Britton [2015] UKSC 36 at [17] and [20]: the natural meaning of the words matters most, and commercial common sense should not be used to undervalue the language.
- **Critique of the Court of Appeal.** Christopher Clarke LJ treated the Hamburg Rules as part of the contractual background ([2015] EWCA Civ 401 at [54]). They were arguably irrelevant, because none of the connecting factors was in a Hamburg Contracting State:
- the port of loading (Nanjing, China);
- the port of discharge (Venezuela);
- the place of issue (probably Hong Kong or the PRC).
- **The PRC merits hearings.** CSAV defended on the merits, alleging fraud and forged documents.
- Neither side proved English law, so Chinese law was applied. Venezuelan law governed whether liability could be waived at the destination port.
- CSAV was held responsible for releasing cargo without the original bills, but Hin-Pro failed to prove loss. The Zhejiang court overturned the Ningbo judgments.
- **Hong Kong Court of Final Appeal.**
- Relying on The Angelic Grace, the court held that no court is offended by an order restraining a party from suing where it promised not to.
- It granted a Mareva injunction in aid of the English judgment. CSAV had a good arguable case, and the PRC claims were brought in breach of contract, in contempt of court and allegedly on forged documents.
- **Hypotheticals raised by Fok PJ.**
- What if the PRC court had positively found the clause non-exclusive? Would English damages for breach of the clause still be convincing?
- What if Hong Kong were asked to enforce a PRC judgment? Hong Kong's Foreign Judgments (Restriction on Recognition and Enforcement) Ordinance (Cap 46) bars recognition of judgments given in breach of dispute-resolution agreements.
- What if both sides sought interim relief in Hong Kong under s 21M?
- Is recovering, as damages, sums paid under PRC judgments consistent with comity?

## The schemes in England
- the Administration of Justice Act 1920 (Commonwealth countries);
- the Foreign Judgments (Reciprocal Enforcement) Act 1933 (listed countries), which is based on common law principles;
- the Civil Jurisdiction and Judgments Act 1982 (other UK jurisdictions, Gibraltar, and Hague 2005 judgments).

## Common law recognition and enforcement
- **Enforcement by action.** A foreign judgment cannot itself be executed in England. The creditor sues on the debt it creates, obtains an English judgment and enforces that. Serving a defendant abroad needs permission under CPR 6.36.
- **Presence** of the defendant in the foreign territory when proceedings were instituted. For companies, presence means a fixed place of business (Adams v Cape Industries [1990] Ch 433).
- Appearing only to contest jurisdiction, to seek a stay in favour of another court or arbitration, or to protect property is not submission (Civil Jurisdiction and Judgments Act 1982, s 33).
- The original cause of action merges and cannot be sued on again (Civil Jurisdiction and Judgments Act 1982, s 34).

## Defences at common law
- It was given in breach of a valid jurisdiction or arbitration agreement (Civil Jurisdiction and Judgments Act 1982, s 32), as English law sees it, even if the foreign court decided otherwise. Only a party who did not agree to the foreign proceedings can rely on this.
- Fraud. Uniquely, it may be raised even if it was or could have been raised abroad: Abouloff v Oppenheimer (1882); Owens Bank v Bracco [1992] 2 AC 443; Takhar v Gracefield [2019] UKSC 13 for domestic judgments.
- Human Rights Act 1998 and Article 6 ECHR (flagrant denial of fair trial). This needs care where non-Convention states are concerned.
- **Judgments against states** (Civil Jurisdiction and Judgments Act 1982, s 31) must be recognisable anyway, and the foreign court must have had jurisdiction consistent with the State Immunity Act 1978, ss 2–11 (submission, commercial transactions and so on).

## Hague Choice of Court Convention 2005: judgments
- **Rule.** Judgments of a court chosen under an exclusive agreement must be recognised and enforced, subject to limited exceptions.
- **Parties include:** EU Member States, the UK, Singapore, Mexico, Montenegro, Ukraine, Moldova and Albania.

## Hague Judgments Convention 2019
- **In the UK.** Inserted into the Civil Jurisdiction and Judgments Act 1982 (s 3F). In force for the UK from 1 July 2025, for proceedings begun on or after that date.
- **Contracting States** at the time of the lecture: the EU (except Denmark), the UK, Ukraine and Uruguay.
- **Scope.**
- "Judgment" means a decision on the merits, including a default judgment. Interim measures are excluded.
- Civil and commercial matters only. Revenue, customs and administrative matters are excluded, as is arbitration (Article 2(3)). Contracting States therefore need not give effect to anti-suit injunctions supporting arbitration.
- Other exclusions include insolvency, carriage of passengers and goods, the validity of legal persons, defamation, privacy and intellectual property.
- **Article 5 filters.** The recognising court decides these for itself:
- residence-type connections of the person against whom recognition is sought (grounds (a), (b), (d));
- consent or submission (grounds (c), (e), (f));
- claim-specific connections, such as the place of contractual performance, tort or immovables (grounds (g) to (l));
- non-exclusive choice of court agreements (ground (m)).
- **Article 7: the exhaustive list of refusal grounds:**
- inadequate notice, or service in the requested state contrary to its public policy;
- fraud;
- manifest incompatibility with public policy;
- proceedings contrary to a choice of court agreement;
- inconsistency with a local judgment between the same parties;
- inconsistency with an earlier recognisable foreign judgment.
- Article 7(2) allows postponement or refusal where earlier local proceedings between the same parties on the same subject matter are pending and closely connected to the requested state.

## International commercial courts
- **Singapore International Commercial Court** (January 2015). Modelled on the English Commercial Court, it aims to be a neutral Asian hub. Its jurisdiction is consent-based, stressing party autonomy.
- **China International Commercial Court** (June 2018). Party autonomy is one basis of its jurisdiction. It forms part of China's policy of increasing its voice in international legal affairs, together with the promotion of its maritime courts.
