# Constitution of trusts
- Milroy v Lord (1862): the settlor must have done everything necessary, according to the nature of the property, to transfer it. Signing transfer forms without completing the bank's required step meant no trust; equity would not treat a failed transfer as a declaration of trust.
- Re Rose [1952]: equity treats the gift as complete when the donor has done everything in his own power, even though registration by a third party happens later. Applied to registered land in Mascall v Mascall (1985).
- T Choithram International v Pagarani [2001] 1 WLR 1 (PC): equity will not strive officiously to defeat a gift; the donor, one of several trustees, constituted the trust by his declaration.
- Pennington v Waine [2002] EWCA Civ 227: the gift was upheld because it had become unconscionable for the donor to revoke, even though she had not done everything in her power. The decision is controversial.
- Strong v Bird: an imperfect gift is perfected if the donee becomes the donor's executor or administrator, provided there was an attempted immediate gift of specific property and the intention continued until death (Re James [1935]).
- Donatio mortis causa: a gift made in contemplation (not necessarily expectation) of death, with delivery of the property or its indicia of title, conditional on death (Cain v Moon [1896]; Sen v Headley [1991] for land). It takes effect through a constructive trust and avoids the Wills Act and LPA formalities.
