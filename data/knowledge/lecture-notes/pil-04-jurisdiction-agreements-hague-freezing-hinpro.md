## Hague Choice of Court Convention 2005
- **Status in the UK.** In force for the UK since 1 October 2015 without a break (the Private International Law (Implementation of Agreements) Act 2020 confirms this). Contracting courts must give effect to exclusive choice of court agreements and recognise the resulting judgments.
- **Conditions for it to apply:**
- **An international case.** A case is not international only if the parties are resident in the same Contracting State and all other relevant elements, apart from the chosen court, are connected only with that State.
- **Timing.** The agreement must have been concluded after the Convention entered into force for the chosen State. The English view is that this was 1 October 2015 for the UK, without a break. The EU has treated agreements made before 1 January 2021 differently, so check both views when a pre-2021 English clause is relied on in an EU court.
- **Form.** In writing, or by other means that make it accessible for later reference.
- **Subject matter.** A civil or commercial matter outside the Article 2 exclusions.
- The exclusions include consumer and employment contracts, family matters, insolvency, carriage of passengers and goods, personal injury claims by individuals, and most IP infringement other than copyright.
- An excluded matter raised only as a preliminary question or a defence does not take the case outside the Convention.
- Insurance and reinsurance are covered (Article 17).
- **Exclusivity.** The agreement must designate one Contracting State's courts exclusively. It is presumed exclusive unless it says otherwise. Non-exclusive and asymmetric clauses fall outside.
- **Validity.** The agreement must be intrinsically valid and consensual. It is separable from the main contract.
- **Scope.** The dispute must fall within the agreement, and the litigants must be bound by it. The Convention is silent on the governing law for this, so English courts apply the law of the contract.
- **Effects.**
- Article 5: the chosen court has jurisdiction unless the agreement is null and void under its own law, and it cannot decline in favour of another State.
- Article 6: a non-chosen court must stay or dismiss unless:
- the agreement is null and void under the chosen court's law;
- a party lacked capacity under the law of the court seised;
- giving effect would cause manifest injustice or be contrary to public policy;
- performance is impossible for reasons beyond the parties' control; or
- the chosen court has declined the case.

## Common law (where the Convention does not apply)
- **An English clause** supports an anti-suit injunction against suit elsewhere, and possibly damages for breach if loss is proved.
- **A foreign clause** gives the defendant an enhanced expectation of a stay ("strong cause" is needed to refuse one).
- **Nature.** A jurisdiction agreement is a contractual promise, but it cannot of itself confer or oust a court's jurisdiction. The question is what the parties agreed in substance.
- **Validity and separability** (Fiona Trust v Privalov [2007] UKHL 40).
- Facts: charterparties allegedly procured by bribery contained English arbitration clauses. The owners claimed to rescind.
- Held:
- rational businesspeople are presumed to want all disputes arising from their relationship decided by the same tribunal unless the wording clearly excludes some;
- under the separability principle (Arbitration Act 1996, s 7), the clause can be attacked only on grounds specific to the clause itself;
- bribery affecting the main contract therefore went to arbitration.
- The same reasoning applies to jurisdiction clauses. They can be attacked where the clause was never agreed (for example a battle of forms) or on grounds specific to it, but not by rescinding the main contract for fraud, misrepresentation or duress.
- **Governing law and interpretation.**
- Interpretation follows the law governing the agreement. For a clause within a contract, that is usually the law of the main contract (under Rome I for contracts after 17 December 2009, and the common law extends it to the clause).
- The law of the chosen forum is not the governing law as such.
- Foreign rules of interpretation are proved by expert evidence (BNP Paribas v Trattamento Rifiuti Metropolitani [2018] EWCA Civ 768).
- On a jurisdiction challenge, foreign law evidence should be limited to differences in principles of construction, not predictions of how the foreign supreme court would rule (Deutsche Bank v Comune di Savona [2018] EWCA Civ 1740).
- Contrast arbitration agreements: Enka v Chubb [2020] UKSC 38 applied the law of the main contract or the closest connection, usually the seat. Arbitration Act 1996, s 6A (added by the Arbitration Act 2025) now applies the law of the seat unless the parties expressly choose otherwise.
- **Scope.**
- English law construes clauses broadly: tort or equitable framing does not escape "all disputes arising under this contract".
- Without express words, clauses do not reach insolvency proceedings or the enforcement of judgments.
- Whether a clause is incorporated from another contract is governed by the law of the incorporating contract.
- Assignees and subrogated insurers take the right subject to the clause.
- **Exclusive or non-exclusive?**
- Decided under the clause's governing law, with English rules applying by default.
- The only question is whether the proceedings complained of breach the promise made.
- There is no presumption either way. Commercial sense and substance matter, not merely the word "exclusive".

## Freezing (Mareva) injunctions
- **Origins.** Nippon Yusen Kaisha v Karageorgis [1975] 1 WLR 1093 and Mareva v International Bulkcarriers [1975] 2 Lloyd's Rep 509 (both Lord Denning MR).
- **Power.** Senior Courts Act 1981, s 37(3) (whether or not the defendant is domiciled, resident or present) assumes the remedy exists rather than creating it (Mercedes Benz v Leiduck [1996] AC 284). The jurisdiction can still develop (Babanaft v Bassatne [1990] Ch 13).
- Civil Jurisdiction and Judgments Act 1982, s 25 allows interim relief for foreign proceedings, extended by s 25(3) to any country.
- To serve a defendant abroad, use the PD 6B para 3.1(5) gateway. CPR 6.37(3) (England as the proper place) still applies.

## Case study: Hin-Pro v CSAV (parallel litigation in England, China and Hong Kong)
- **Facts.** CSAV, a Chilean carrier, issued 70 straight bills of lading to Hin-Pro (Hong Kong) for shipments from China to Venezuela. Hin-Pro alleged delivery without production of the bills.
- **Clause 23.**
- "Shall be subject to English law and the jurisdiction of the English High Court".
- If proceedings are nonetheless brought elsewhere, they go to the ordinary courts.
- In Chile, no arbitration.
- **The litigation.**
- Hin-Pro sued in several PRC maritime courts (Wuhan, Guangzhou, Qingdao, Tianjin, Ningbo, Shanghai).
- CSAV obtained English anti-suit injunctions, a contempt order and sequestration. It then obtained a permanent injunction, damages (including repayment of sums paid under a Ningbo judgment that was later reversed) and a worldwide freezing order.
- Chinese courts rejected CSAV's jurisdiction challenges. Qingdao relied on a lack of actual connection. Ningbo held the clause non-exclusive because it did not forbid suing elsewhere.
- **English Court of Appeal** ([2015] EWCA Civ 401) held the clause exclusive:
- it is interpreted by English law in English, between commercial parties, not consumers;
- "shall be subject to" is imperative and "transitive", submitting all disputes to England, and is stronger than the wording in Svendborg;
- since English law is plainly mandatory, the same applies to jurisdiction, and England is the best forum for its own law;
- "notwithstanding the foregoing" in the second sentence presupposes an obligation to litigate in England. The second and third sentences cater for jurisdictions that ignore exclusive clauses (Hamburg Rules states, China);
- contra proferentem is of little help in commercial contracts;
- authority tends to read such clauses as exclusive.
- Contrast Import Export Metro v CSAV [2003] EWHC 11 (Comm), where an identical clause was held non-exclusive. CSAV itself had then argued for non-exclusivity, but its stay application still failed.
- **Hong Kong Court of Final Appeal** ([2016] HKCFA 79).
- The lower courts refused a Mareva injunction and a receiver to avoid taking sides between England and the PRC.
- The Court of Final Appeal allowed the appeal. Enforcing an English damages award for breach of an exclusive clause is not a breach of comity or public policy, so a Mareva injunction in aid of the English judgment was available.
- **Discussion points.** Is the Court of Appeal's reading convincing? Is the Hong Kong ruling right? The case shows how divergent interpretations of one clause create conflicts between jurisdictions.
