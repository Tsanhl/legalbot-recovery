## Shared occupation and joint tenancy
- Contrast Antoniades v Villiers [1990] (a couple sharing a one-bedroom flat under separate but interdependent "licences": joint tenants) with AG Securities v Vaughan [1990] (four occupiers with separate agreements, arriving at different times: no exclusive possession, no tenancy). Separate but identical or interdependent documents, "no collective exclusive possession" wording and individual rent shares are labels that do not decide the question.

## Term certain and rent
- The start and maximum duration must be certain at the outset (Lace v Chantler [1944]; Prudential Assurance v London Residuary Body [1992]; Berrisford v Mexfield Housing Co-operative [2011] UKSC 52, where an uncertain term took effect as a 90-year lease determinable on death). Ashburn Anstalt v Arnold [1989] departed briefly from this.
- Rent is usual but not essential (LPA 1925, s 205(1)(xxvii): "whether or not at a rent"; Ashburn). It may be money or money's worth, need not be a fixed sum if ascertainable by a formula, and service charges are not rent (Bostock v Bryant (1990)).

## Types of lease
- Fixed term; periodic (weekly, monthly or yearly, ended by notice matching the period). A periodic tenancy may be implied from possession and rent paid at intervals, but not while parties are still negotiating (Javad v Aqil [1991]); there is no presumption from payment of rent, and the court looks objectively at all the facts (Barclays Wealth Trustees v Erimus Housing [2014] EWCA Civ 303; Smoke Club v Network Rail). Also tenancies at will, at sufferance and by estoppel, and the non-proprietary "Bruton" tenancy (Bruton v London & Quadrant Housing Trust [2000]).

## Formalities and third parties
- Legal lease: 3 years or less can be oral if it takes effect in possession at the best rent (LPA 1925, s 54(2)); longer leases need a deed (s 52); over 7 years must be registered (LRA 2002, s 27(2)), and in unregistered land its grant triggers first registration.
- Effect on third parties in registered land: leases of 3 years or less cannot be noted but override (Sch 1/3 para 1); leases of 3–7 years can be noted and also override; leases over 7 years bind once registered, otherwise they are equitable at best.
- Equitable lease (Walsh v Lonsdale (1882)): a valid contract (in writing, containing all the terms, signed by both parties: LP(MP)A 1989, s 2) plus the availability of specific performance (consideration, damages inadequate, clean hands; rent arrears can bar it: Rouf v Tragus Holdings [2009]). In registered land it needs a notice or actual occupation; in unregistered land it is a Class C(iv) estate contract, void against a purchaser if unregistered.

## Covenants
- Quiet enjoyment (implied) protects possession from interference by the landlord or its agents, not general noise (Jenkins v Jackson (1888)), and does not cover the property's state at the start (Southwark LBC v Mills [2001]: no soundproofing duty). Breaches include removing doors or windows (Lavender v Betts [1942]), cutting off supplies (Perera v Vandiyar [1953]), unmitigated building works (Timothy Taylor v Mayfair House [2016]) and harassment (Kenny v Preen [1963]).
- Non-derogation from grant: the landlord must not undermine the purpose known at the grant (Aldin v Latimer Clark [1894]); letting next door to a competitor is not a breach; specialist uses need express terms (Robinson v Kilvert (1889)).
- Repair and fitness: furnished lettings must be fit at the start (Smith v Marrable (1843); no continuing duty: Sarson v Roberts [1895]); common parts in multi-occupation must be maintained (Liverpool CC v Irwin [1977]). For residential leases under 7 years, the landlord must keep the structure, exterior and installations for water, gas, electricity, sanitation and heating in repair (Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, s 11), judged by the property's age, character and locality, once notified (O'Brien v Robinson [1973]). It covers walls, floors and windows but not condensation from design defects (Quick v Taff Ely BC [1986]; Lee v Leeds CC [2002]) or paths beyond the building (Edwards v Kumarasamy [2016] UKSC 40); an ineffective damp remedy may still breach (Uddin v Islington LBC [2015]). The Homes (Fitness for Human Habitation) Act 2018 implies fitness at the start and throughout, judged by s 10 factors and the 29 hazards in the Housing Health and Safety Rating System regulations; remedies are specific performance and damages.
- Tenant: pay rent (implied if not expressed); keep the premises in tenantable repair allowing for fair wear and tear; not commit waste (Mancetter Developments v Garmanson [1986]); use the premises in a tenant-like manner, doing the little jobs a reasonable tenant would (Warren v Keen [1954]).

## Termination and forfeiture
- A lease ends by expiry, break clause (Mannai v Eagle Star [1997]), notice to quit for periodic tenancies (one joint tenant's notice suffices: Hammersmith & Fulham LBC v Monk [1992]), surrender, merger, repudiatory breach, frustration or forfeiture. Death does not end a lease.
- Forfeiture for unpaid rent: a formal demand (unless waived by the lease or the tenant is six months in arrears), then possession proceedings (Common Law Procedure Act 1852, ss 210–212); relief under the County Courts Act 1984, s 138 or equity.
- Forfeiture for other breaches: a LPA 1925, s 146 notice specifying the breach, requiring remedy if remediable and compensation if sought. The remediability test is whether the harm can be removed (Expert Clothing v Hillgate House [1986]); unlawful subletting (Scala House v Forbes [1974]) and immoral use (Rugby School v Tannahill [1935]) are irremediable. Relief under s 146(2) is discretionary, weighing the seriousness of the breach, the parties' conduct, the tenant's past performance, the draconian nature of forfeiture, and the harm against the lease's value (Ropemaker Properties v Noonhaven [1989]).
