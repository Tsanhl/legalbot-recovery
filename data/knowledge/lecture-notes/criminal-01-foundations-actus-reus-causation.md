## Foundations
- The prosecution proves guilt beyond reasonable doubt (Woolmington v DPP [1935] AC 462, the "golden thread"). Defences usually carry an evidential burden (raise enough to make it a live issue; the prosecution then disproves it); some reverse the legal burden onto the defendant on the balance of probabilities (Carr-Briant [1943]).
- Offences are indictable (Crown Court: murder, manslaughter, rape, OAPA s 18), either-way (s 47, s 20, sexual assault) or summary (common assault and battery).

## Structure of an offence
- Actus reus: conduct, circumstances and results. Mens rea may be required for each element. Offences may be conduct crimes (theft), circumstance or state-of-affairs crimes (being in charge of a vehicle while unfit through drink: Road Traffic Act 1988, s 4(2)), or result crimes (murder). Defences sit alongside.

## Omissions
- Duties arise from family relationships (Gibbins and Proctor (1918)), contract (Pittwood (1902)), creating a dangerous situation (Miller [1983] 2 AC 161), and others such as voluntary assumption of care. The duty requires only reasonable steps (Miller; Evans [2009] EWCA Crim 650).
- Causation for omissions: the result must be one that the omitted act would (not merely might) have prevented (Morby (1882); Broughton [2020] EWCA Crim 1093).

## Causation (result crimes)
- Factual causation: "but for" (White [1910]: poison not the cause of death, so attempted murder only). D's conduct need only contribute more than negligibly (Dyson [1908]; Hennigan (1971); L [2010] EWCA Crim 1249); several causes can coexist (Warburton and Hubbersty [2006] EWCA Crim 627: did D's acts significantly contribute?).
- Legal causation: D's conduct must be a substantial (more than de minimis: Cato [1976]), blameworthy (Dalloway (1847)) and operating cause, with no novus actus interveniens. Experts inform the jury but do not decide causation (Kimel [2016]).
- Third parties: instinctive self-preservation or acts in the line of duty do not break the chain (Pagett (1983)); reasonably foreseeable acts do not (Girdler [2020] EWCA Crim 407); free, deliberate and informed acts do.
- Medical treatment breaks the chain only if palpably wrong and so independent and potent that D's contribution is insignificant (Jordan (1956), exceptional; Smith [1959]; Cheshire [1991]).
- Victims: D takes V as found, including beliefs (Blaue [1975]: refusal of a transfusion; Holland (1841)); self-neglect does not break the chain (Wall (1802)); escape attempts do not unless "daft" or unforeseeable (Roberts (1972); Williams and Davies [1992]). A free, deliberate, informed choice to self-administer drugs breaks the chain (Kennedy (No 2) [2007] UKHL 38), but not where the victim's choice was not truly free (Wallace [2018] EWCA Crim 690: euthanasia after acid attack; Field [2021] EWCA Crim 380; compare Rebelo [2021] EWCA Crim 306).
